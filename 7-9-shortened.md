# Automated Red Teaming for Generative Models: Shortened draft of Sections 7--9

This working draft shortens Sections 7--9 of the survey while retaining the original claims, examples, and citation keys. Citations remain in LaTeX form so they can be compared directly with the source manuscript.

## 7. Failure remediation and safety improvement

### 7.1 Learning from red-team failures

Red-team traces identify conditions under which a target model produces harmful or otherwise undesirable behavior. They can therefore supply evidence for safety tuning, rather than serving only as test cases. Ganguli et al. frame red teaming as a process of discovering, measuring, and reducing harmful outputs, and release 38,961 attacks for later analysis \cite{ganguli2022redteaming}. Perez et al. show that one language model can generate tests for another and uncover large numbers of offensive replies \cite{perez2022redteaming}. MART closes this loop by generating adversarial prompts, fine-tuning the target with safety-aligned data built around them, and attacking the updated target \cite{ge2024mart}.

Recent work likewise reuses generated attacks as safety-training data. Learning Diverse Attacks evaluates resulting models against other reinforcement-learning-based red-teamers, while DiveR-CT relates the diversity and composition of collected attacks to downstream blue-team tuning \cite{lee2025diverseattacks,zhao2025diverct}. Once failures are reused for updates, the red-team generator helps define the model's evolving safety-training distribution.

Targeted supervision can improve measured safety while degrading benign behavior. Safety-Tuned LLaMAs finds that roughly 3\% safety examples can improve safety without significant degradation on standard capability and helpfulness benchmarks, but more extensive tuning can cause refusals of superficially similar safe prompts \cite{bianchi2024safetytuned}. RASS generates and selects prompts near the safety boundary to mitigate over-refusal, and XSTest measures this problem with 250 safe prompts and 200 unsafe contrasts \cite{pan2025overrefusal,rottger2024xstest}. Harmful-query refusal alone is therefore not an adequate remediation metric.

A fixed set of failure traces cannot show how vulnerabilities change after an update. MART regenerates attacks against the revised target in each round and reports up to an 84.7\% reduction in violation rate after four rounds while helpfulness on non-adversarial prompts remains stable \cite{ge2024mart}. HarmBench's R2D2 similarly maintains persistent test cases that are dynamically optimized during training \cite{mazeika2024harmbench}. The diversity and composition of generated attacks also affect downstream safety tuning, even in studies that do not implement the same attacker--defender loop \cite{lee2025diverseattacks,zhao2025diverct}.

This feedback loop does not establish general robustness. Jailbroken identifies competing objectives and mismatched generalization as failure modes for safety training \cite{wei2023jailbroken}. Simple Adaptive Attacks reports 100\% success on several tested safety-aligned models, including R2D2, after adapting the attack to the target \cite{andriushchenko2025adaptive}. In another study, past-tense reformulation increased the reported success rate on GPT-4o from 1\% for direct requests to 88\% after 20 attempts \cite{andriushchenko2025pasttense}. Adding examples of that reformulation can improve its specific defense without resolving the broader generalization problem. Safety Reasoning with Guidelines similarly finds that direct refusal training can rely on superficial mappings under out-of-distribution attacks \cite{wang2025safetyreasoning}.

Remediation should therefore be evaluated on held-out harmful behaviors, unseen or adaptive attacks, and benign utility. HarmBench and JailbreakBench support reproducible comparison, but neither is a complete measure of deployment risk \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. XSTest and Safety-Tuned LLaMAs provide separate evidence on over-refusal \cite{rottger2024xstest,bianchi2024safetytuned}. Confirmed failures should also be retained as regression cases.

### 7.2 Turning safety judgments into runtime control

Training-time mitigation changes model parameters; inference-time mitigation uses safety judgments to control generation while leaving them fixed or largely unchanged. A system can apply these signals before generation, during generation, or after generation.

At input and output boundaries, evaluator-like models can inspect user prompts, generated responses, or both. WildGuard, Llama Guard, and ShieldGemma provide prompt or response moderation capabilities, while NeMo Guardrails connects moderation decisions to programmable runtime rails \cite{han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,zeng2024shieldgemma,rebedea2023nemo}. Once such a model enters the deployment path, its prediction becomes an intervention signal. This requires calibration and threshold evaluation as well as aggregate accuracy: LLM guard models can be overconfident, miscalibrated under jailbreaks, and variable across response models \cite{liu2025guardcalibration}.

Before generation, systems can analyze intent, rewrite a prompt, add policy guidance, or route a request through a restricted flow. Intention Analysis first infers an input's essential intent and conditions a subsequent response on that interpretation \cite{zhang2025intention}. During generation, SafeDecoding changes decoding probabilities with token-level safety information, while Jailbreak Antidote steers selected hidden states along a safety direction \cite{xu2024safedecoding,shen2025jailbreakantidote}. After generation, output guards can block or route unsafe content \cite{han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,rebedea2023nemo}. These mechanisms differ in access requirements, latency, controllability, and architectural dependence.

Attackers and defenses can both use feedback to alter later generation, but toward opposite objectives. SafeDecoding and Immune use token-level safety signals or rewards; Intention Analysis and Reasoning-to-Defend use an intermediate interpretation or safety reflections to shape a later response \cite{xu2024safedecoding,ghosal2025immune,zhang2025intention,zhu2025reasoningtodefend}. This shared feedback structure does not imply a common implementation: several defenses depend on target-specific decoding or trained behavior rather than a model-agnostic judge-and-regenerate wrapper.

Multimodal systems require guards to match the location of the risk signal. A text-only guard can miss malicious images, image--text interactions, or modality-specific encodings. CIDER detects perturbed image inputs through cross-modal similarity and operates independently of the target MLLM \cite{xu2024cider}. Llama Guard 3 Vision and MULTIGUARD extend moderation across image, language, and audio inputs, while Immune uses a multimodal safety reward during decoding \cite{chi2024llamaguardvision,verma2025multiguard,ghosal2025immune}. These methods cover cross-modal input detection, multimodal moderation, and decoding-time guidance; they do not provide complete coverage of generated media.

For text-to-image and text-to-video systems, risk can emerge during generation or become visible only in the output. SAFREE detects toxic concepts in text-embedding space, steers diffusion away from unsafe subspaces, and self-validates during sampling \cite{yoon2025safree}. T2VShield combines prompt rewriting with temporal and cross-modal analysis \cite{liang2025t2vshield}. TEAR shows why static prompt filters are insufficient: innocuous prompts can exploit temporal event sequencing to elicit policy-violating video content \cite{he2026tear}. Audio presents a related gap because risk may reside in semantic content or waveform-level signals. SALMONN-Guard and AudioGuard focus on input-side interception, while generated-audio moderation remains comparatively underrepresented \cite{yang2026salmonnguard,kang2026audioguard}.

Guardrails are themselves part of the attack surface. Their evaluation should include calibration, benign utility, runtime cost, and robustness to adaptive or transferred attacks, not only accuracy on a fixed set. Confirmed bypasses can become regression cases for continuous red teaming.

### 7.3 Securing the application beyond the model

Model-level safety does not establish system-level safety. When a model connects to retrieval, tools, persistent state, and external services, a failure can alter control flow, exercise an unauthorized capability, disclose data, or change external state. AgentDojo evaluates security properties and tool consequences rather than final text alone \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}.

Application design must keep information that may inform a task separate from authority that may redefine it. In indirect prompt injection, the critical failure is allowing untrusted content to obtain control authority. CaMeL extracts control and data flows from the trusted query, constrains untrusted retrieved content's influence on program flow, and uses capabilities to limit unauthorized information flows \cite{debenedetti2025camel}.

Independent execution controls can limit damage even after an injection alters model behavior. IsolateGPT isolates application execution and mediates access through controlled interfaces and permissions \cite{wu2025isolategpt}. Task Shield checks whether instructions and proposed tool calls serve the user's specified goals, while ToolSafe detects unsafe calls before execution and feeds that judgment back into the agent's reasoning \cite{jia2025taskshield,mou2026toolsafe}. The application, not the model, should determine whether a proposed action is authorized. AuthBench reports both omitted permissions needed for tasks and grants of unused or sensitive access; additional inference-time reasoning does not remove this mismatch \cite{do2026leastprivilege}.

Application-level evaluation should measure unauthorized actions, data exfiltration, and protected-state changes, not textual behavior alone. AgentDojo and ToolEmu respectively evaluate realistic tool workflows and high-stakes tool-use failures in an LM-emulated sandbox \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,ruan2024toolemu}. Prevention and isolation are better developed than recovery after side effects. Once an agent sends an email, changes a permission, or writes to an external service, the effect may be irreversible or outside the application's control. Cordon explores staged effects and commit/rollback boundaries, but evidence for general recovery remains limited \cite{chen2026cordon}.

System-level red teaming should test whether untrusted data gains control authority, a compromised model gains unauthorized capabilities, and prohibited effects are executed or contained. Adaptive attacks against system controls and recovery from durable side effects remain under-tested.

### 7.4 Continuous testing after remediation

Continuous red teaming turns one-time remediation into a replayable workflow. A replayable failure case should preserve the observed behavior, target configuration, evaluator, threat condition, and reproduction context; severity and deduplication metadata can support triage. HarmBench and JailbreakBench provide reusable behaviors, artifacts, models, defenses, wrappers, and scoring protocols, but neither is a complete software-style regression system \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}.

Regression replay must be version-aware. A failure can disappear, recur, or change in severity after a model or mitigation update. The GPT-4 System Card describes iterative red teaming across versions and control layers \cite{openai2023gpt4systemcard}. For model-based applications, the replay target is the full evaluated configuration: policies, retrieval, tools, evaluators, and execution environment can all change the observed outcome.

Retained regression cases and renewed adversarial discovery answer different questions. Retained cases test recurrence of known failures. AutoRedTeamer renews attack selection through memory-guided strategy proposal, whereas Jailbreak Distillation renews the benchmark by distilling attacks into updateable safety artifacts \cite{zhou2025autoredteamer,zhang2025jbdistill}. Passing a retained suite does not establish broad safety coverage.

A mature loop combines persistence, which detects recurrence, with renewal, which probes beyond known failures \cite{openai2023gpt4systemcard,zhou2025autoredteamer,zhang2025jbdistill}. It should also track changes to taxonomies, judges, and evaluation targets so that longitudinal results remain comparable.

## 8. Practice, tools, and responsible use

### 8.1 Operationalizing red teaming with evaluation harnesses

Evaluation harnesses connect target adapters, attack or probe modules, evaluators, execution controls, and result logging. garak, PyRIT, Inspect AI, and promptfoo each provide this integration layer across targets, probes, runs, and recorded outputs or scores \cite{derczynski2024garak,lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect,promptfooDocs}. Their common contribution is an executable workflow, not a particular jailbreak method.

Harnesses differ in the interaction scope they execute. PyRIT supports modular attacks and multimodal targets, while Inspect AI exposes tasks, solvers, tools, scorers, sandboxes, and execution logs \cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect}. AgentDojo and CybersecurityBenchmarks illustrate environments in which success depends on tool use or application-level behavior \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,metaCybersecurityBenchmarks}. The practical question is how much of the target system enters the executable evaluation loop.

Benchmarks stabilize what is tested; harnesses organize how it is executed. MM-SafetyBench provides fixed multimodal safety artifacts \cite{liu2024mmsafetybench}. PyRIT and Inspect AI expose replaceable targets, attacks, evaluators, and execution components \cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect}. HarmBench and JailbreakBench combine both roles through standardized artifacts, threat models, scoring functions, wrappers, and extensible attack methods \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}.

Logging, reproducibility, and regression are distinct levels of persistence. Logging records a run; reproducibility retains enough identifiers, configuration, seeds, or artifacts to rerun or compare it; regression deliberately re-executes retained tests after a change. The reviewed systems expose different combinations of these mechanisms \cite{derczynski2024garak,openaiEvals,promptfooDocs,mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,debenedetti2024agentdojo,metaCybersecurityBenchmarks,liang2023helm}. Inspect AI is explicit about persistent logs, saved configuration, and re-scoring \cite{ukaisi2024inspect}. Documentation more consistently describes logging and reruns than complete regression workflows.

### 8.2 Where human expertise still matters

Human participation expands the risk space by changing what is tested. Adversarial Nibbler used crowdsourced red teaming to surface implicit adversarial text-to-image prompts, long-tail issues, and novel strategies \cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler}. Going PLACES elicited culturally and linguistically situated prompts and annotations in Ghana, Nigeria, Karnataka, and Punjab; CulturalBench combined human-written and human-verified questions across 45 global regions \cite{rastogi2026goingplaces,chiu-etal-2025-culturalbench}. STAR uses human-authored, parameterized risk rules, and a participatory study in South Korea links lived experience to stereotype-targeted strategies \cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,kim2026darkbright}.

The recurring division of labor is complementary. Humans define risks, seeds, contexts, and priorities; automated systems generate, execute, and screen tests at scale \cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart,perez2022redteaming,quaye2026bridging,alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}. AART encodes context, policy, harms, populations, regions, and priorities as recipes for a language model to expand. Bridging the Scale Gap expands human-authored seeds with human-derived strategies \cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart,quaye2026bridging}. Perez et al. find that optimizing attack effectiveness can reduce diversity, so automation does not automatically broaden coverage \cite{perez2022redteaming}. Scaling generation also shifts the bottleneck toward judgment.

Human review can validate an automated judge on a bounded sample, interpret uncertain or context-sensitive cases, and adjudicate disagreement. Perez et al. compare an offensive-language classifier with human annotations \cite{perez2022redteaming}. Build it Break it Fix it, Going PLACES, and Adversarial Nibbler show why conversational and local context can matter \cite{dinan-etal-2019-build,rastogi2026goingplaces,quaye2024adversarialnibbler}. STAR, DICES, and D3CODE preserve reasoning or structured disagreement rather than treating a single human label as unquestionable ground truth \cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,NEURIPS2023_a74b697b,mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code}. The appropriate escalation criteria remain unresolved.

Human red teaming is costly to apply across large or changing test spaces. Hybrid workflows reserve human effort for risk definition and difficult judgments while using automation for high-volume generation and screening \cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart,perez2022redteaming,alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}.

### 8.3 Balancing transparency and exploitability

Automated red teaming produces evaluation logs, benchmark cases, optimized prompts, attacker models, and reproduction triggers. These materials support scrutiny but can lower the cost of misuse. External red teaming can expose novel jailbreaks or harmful-content techniques, and offensive-security evaluations show that this risk can become operational \cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack}.

Artifact sensitivity depends on operational reusability: actionability, reusability, and the expertise or search effort needed for exploitation. File type alone is insufficient. Qi et al. gated realistic harmful benchmark questions while reporting results on public AdvBench; offensive-security studies restricted prompts, source documents, or implementations while reporting evaluations \cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}.

Release practices vary by content, access, timing, and disclosure process. Studies have withheld harmful evaluation sets, restricted implementation details or target identities, and disclosed sensitive findings directly to providers \cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}. GPT-2's staged release provides a precedent for changing availability as risk assessment evolves, but not a ready-made red-team standard \cite{solaiman2019release}. Restricted artifacts can still support audit when authors document the threat model, setup, procedure, and representative non-sensitive evidence. Public Cybench, HarmBench, and Ganguli et al.'s dataset show when scoped artifacts can support inspection and comparison, but they do not imply that directly reusable exploit chains should be released \cite{zhang2025cybenchframeworkevaluatingcybersecurity,mazeika2024harmbench,ganguli2022redteaming}.

Reports should preserve the trace from threat model and attack generation through execution, evaluation, and observed failure. They should report relevant versions, criteria, and costs; if remediation is claimed, they should also report the mitigation and a held-out regression result. When materials are restricted, authors should identify what remains available for independent scrutiny. Shared rules for access, timing, maintenance, and accountability remain a field-level governance problem.

## 9. Open problems and research agenda

### 9.1 Coverage beyond fixed benchmarks

Coverage raises four linked questions: **representation** defines the denominator; **identity** defines a distinct failure; **discovery dynamics** measure the marginal yield of new failures; and **stopping confidence** concerns failures that may remain unseen. A coverage claim is meaningful only after its representation and identity rule are specified.

Existing benchmarks usually adopt closed-world representations. HarmBench and JailbreakBench cover predefined harmful behaviors, SALAD-Bench covers a safety taxonomy, and HELM covers scenario or metric spaces \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,li2024salad,liang2023helm}. These choices support standardized comparison within their selected space. MultiMon offers an open-world direction by deriving systematic multimodal failure categories from observations, but the size and structure of the remaining failure space remain unspecified \cite{tong2023multimon}.

Prompt diversity, attack diversity, and failure diversity are not equivalent. Different prompts or strategies can instantiate the same failure, while similar observations can reveal different mechanisms. Failure identity may be defined at the instance, semantic-category, interaction-pattern, or mechanism-and-context level. Each definition produces a different deduplication unit and coverage estimate. MultiMon links instances to broader categories, and Quality Diversity provides an analogy in which distinctness depends on behavior descriptors or niches \cite{tong2023multimon,pugh2016qualitydiversity}. Without a stable identity rule, novelty and marginal discovery have no clear unit.

Current attack studies report query budgets, pruning, iterative refinement, population sizes, and related measures of search efficiency \cite{yu2024llmfuzzer,mehrotra2024tap,chao2025pair,liu2024autodan}. Coverage instead requires discovery efficiency: the rate at which distinct failures appear as budget increases. A declining discovery curve may reflect exhausted reachable failures or a search trapped in one region. Without a model of reachability, it does not establish coverage saturation.

Stopping claims require a defensible statement about unseen failures. The sources reviewed here do not provide an automated-red-teaming stopping criterion for this purpose. Chao's estimator for the number of classes in a population is a statistical analogue, not a plug-in solution \cite{chao1984unseenclasses}. A red-teaming analogue would need distinct observed failure classes, an account of adaptive sampling, and a severity-aware uncertainty statement. Adaptive search, dependent observations, unstable identities, heterogeneous severity, and changing models or environments all complicate that goal.

### 9.2 Reliable judgment under adversarial search

Automated red teaming is only as valid as its success judgments. JailbreakBench reports substantial false-positive and false-negative variation across rule-based, general-purpose, and specialized judges; StrongREJECT finds that evaluator choice can change both estimated jailbreak effectiveness and attack rankings \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench,souly2024strongreject}. These discrepancies matter more on adversarially searched outputs, which may differ from the data used to validate the evaluator. Human audits find accuracy variation by attack type, victim model, and harm category, in some slices approaching chance; correcting errors can change the apparent success of attacks \cite{schwinn2026coinflip}. Other audits show that surface wrapping or a refusal prefix can reverse LLM-judge decisions without changing harmful content \cite{gao2026reliablejailbreakjudge}.

In static evaluation, judge error corrupts the reported result. In automated search, it can also control selection, refinement, pruning, and termination. Tree of Attacks places a judge inside an iterative attack tree \cite{mehrotra2024tap}. Direct attacks on LLM judges can induce attacker-controlled candidate selection \cite{shi2024optimization}, and red-team-specific evidence shows that judge-guided optimization can increase measured success by exploiting false positives without increasing human-rated harmfulness \cite{schwinn2026coinflip}. When search-time scores control candidate survival, a judge weakness becomes a weakness in the search objective.

Search and final reporting should use separate evaluators where possible, and the reporting judge should be audited on samples from the evolving attack distribution. JailbreakBench partly operationalizes this separation by prohibiting optimization against its leaderboard judge \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}. Calibration, evaluator disagreement, and adversarial stress tests are candidate signals for escalating ambiguous or high-risk cases to independent judges or targeted human review \cite{jung2024trust}. The problem is harder when a failure depends on multimodal or action-based evidence rather than one text response \cite{treeteaming2026}.

### 9.3 Testing stateful and long-horizon failures

State persistence concerns information that remains available across steps, such as memory, retrieval, tool or application state, authority, prior actions, and supplied context. Interaction horizon concerns the depth of dependent interaction before a safety outcome is determined. AgentPoison and PoisonedRAG illustrate persistence through poisoned long-term memory or retrieval stores \cite{chen2024agentpoison,zou2025poisonedrag}. Agent-security benchmarks extend the same issue to tool outputs, application state, and external context \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhang2025asb,zhan2024injecagent,abdelnabi2023indirect}.

When attacker choices depend on earlier observations or state, the attack is an interaction trajectory rather than an isolated input. MUSE uses tree search over multi-turn dialogues conditioned on history and previous responses \cite{yan2025muse}. The evaluation must then score actions, security properties, state transitions, or downstream consequences. AgentDojo, Agent Security Bench, InjecAgent, ToolEmu, tau-bench, and OSWorld provide examples of these outcomes and environments \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhang2025asb,zhan2024injecagent,ruan2024toolemu,yao2025taubench,xie2024osworld}.

Meaningful testing requires an environment that exposes the relevant state transitions and consequences. AgentDojo and WebInject instantiate adversarial tool or web-agent settings; ToolEmu provides an emulated sandbox; WebArena, OSWorld, and tau-bench provide executable tasks with observable state \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,wang2025webinject,ruan2024toolemu,zhou2024webarena,xie2024osworld,yao2025taubench}. The open challenge is to combine explicit security objectives, mutable state, adaptive trajectories, and auditable success criteria in one evaluation setting.

### 9.4 Transfer beyond the original target

Transfer should specify both the object that transfers and the boundary that changes. **Artifact-level transfer** means that a concrete suffix or jailbreak prompt works on a new target. **Strategy-level transfer** means that a generation or search principle remains useful after target-specific artifacts are regenerated. **Failure-level transfer** means that the underlying failure remains meaningful and reproducible. Relevant boundaries include changes to the model or alignment procedure, changes in language, modality, encoding, or format for a preserved intent, and changes to the deployed system. Failure-level transfer requires comparable source and target conditions.

Across models, transfer is strongly source--target dependent. GCG transfers some optimized suffixes to unseen open models and proprietary interfaces, but reports weaker success on others and source overfitting with longer optimization \cite{zou2023gcg}. AutoDAN reports stronger transfer than GCG in some settings but asymmetric source--target results and low transfer to the tested GPT-4 version \cite{liu2024autodan}. AmpleGCG also reports transfer to unseen targets under target- and protocol-dependent conditions \cite{liao2024amplegcg}. A broader study finds poor and inconsistent transfer across 13 open models \cite{meade2025triggers}. Artifact-level transfer should therefore be reported as a directed relation, not as a property of a jailbreak in isolation.

The same harmful intent can yield different outcomes when its representation changes. Native-speaker translations produced different unsafe rates across nine non-English languages in evaluations of ChatGPT and GPT-4 \cite{deng2024multilingual}. Con Instruction applies one construction principle to separately optimized image and audio inputs, demonstrating strategy-level cross-modal generalization rather than direct artifact reuse \cite{geng2025coninstruction}. Comparisons must preserve intent, document the transformation, and use semantically comparable criteria; otherwise, translation or modality-specific judging may be mistaken for transfer.

Textual harm, harmful system behavior, and executable harmful policies are distinct outcomes. HouYi finds successful prompt-injection scenarios in 31 of 36 tested LLM-integrated applications, but application logic and interfaces interrupt portability elsewhere \cite{liu2023houyi}. Jailbreak-Zero transfers selected attacks across victim families and to safety policies unseen during attacker fine-tuning \cite{hu2026jailbreakzero}. POEX shows that accepted or harmful-looking text-centric policies may not be executable in embodied systems \cite{lu2025poex}. Transfer reports should state the source--target boundary, transferred object, and whether the risk objective and success condition are preserved or explicitly mapped.

### 9.5 Comparing safety as evaluations evolve

Standardized evaluations make comparison possible by specifying harmful behaviors, threat models, artifacts, judges, attack settings, and reporting conventions \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. Continual evaluation updates some of these elements as new failures, capabilities, applications, and policies emerge. Longitudinal interpretation must distinguish **system drift** in the tested model, application, guardrails, or configuration; **evaluation-target drift** in the safety property; and **evaluation-procedure drift** in case distribution, attack policy, judge, budget, or configuration.

ASR is a conditional measurement under a specified target definition, case distribution, search policy, judge, and budget. Generation settings, evaluator choices, attack choices, and resource accounting can change it even under shared benchmark conventions \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. h4rm3l and Tree of Attacks further show how search-space transformations, branching, pruning, depth, width, and query use affect which attacks are discovered \cite{doumbouya2025h4rm3l,mehrotra2024treeofattacks}. A change in ASR therefore cannot automatically be attributed to deterioration of the target system.

Adaptive evaluation makes this especially clear. AgenticEval generates and intensifies cases over successive rounds under a fixed policy framework and target, so its measured safety rate can fall as the procedure changes \cite{wang2026agenticeval}. DAS likewise finds vulnerabilities missed by static benchmarks through dynamic adversarial auditing of medically grounded cases \cite{pan2026dynamicredteaming}. Adaptive methods can improve discovery while changing the context in which scores are measured.

Evolving scores require an explicit comparability argument. Dynabench discusses retaining earlier rounds alongside high-quality static sets, and LiveBench provides versioned refresh and model re-runs \cite{kiela2021dynabench,white2025livebench}. A practical design combines an evolving discovery suite with a stable anchor set, versioned system, target, and procedure definitions, and selective old/new cross-evaluation. The anchor will not identify every source of drift, but it makes comparability testable.

### 9.6 Governing shared red-team evidence

Governance matters when artifacts, evaluation access, and disclosure decisions cross organizational boundaries. Researchers, providers, benchmark maintainers, and independent evaluators may disagree about access or disclosure. Public red-teaming practice varies; controlled access, responsible disclosure, staged release, and coordinated flaw disclosure provide partial precedents rather than a shared protocol \cite{feffer2024redteaming,ahmad2025externalredteaming,solaiman2019release,cattell2024coordinatedflaw}. A field-level process must assign decision rights, preserve records, and provide a route for verification and adjudication.

Shared benchmarks, judges, regression cases, and attack libraries also require maintenance and version governance. Medical LLM benchmark work, Dynabench, and LiveBench highlight documentation, openness, maintenance responsibility, feedback channels, periodic refresh, and versioned evaluation \cite{wang2026beyondleaderboard,kiela2021dynabench,white2025livebench}. Automated red teaming needs comparable provenance and version histories for cases, judges, and attack artifacts, along with rules for replacing obsolete elements while retaining stable regression sets.

High-severity findings require a path from discovery to verification, provider notification, remediation, and, when appropriate, public disclosure. Zhu et al. withheld code and prompts while using a provider's responsible-disclosure channel \cite{zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}. Public-practice analysis and coordinated-flaw-disclosure proposals identify possible verification, coordination, and adjudication mechanisms \cite{feffer2024redteaming,cattell2024coordinatedflaw}. Severity assessment, remediation tracking, disclosure timing, and audit records may need different rules for different classes of findings.

## Conclusion

Automated red teaming is a closed-loop safety-evaluation system, not a collection of isolated jailbreak techniques. A useful evaluation connects risk specification, generation, interaction, judgment, coverage, mitigation, operational practice, and responsible governance across generative-model targets.
