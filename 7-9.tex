\begin{abstract}
The rapid deployment of generative models has created a need for automated red-teaming methods that discover failures beyond manually authored test cases. This survey focuses on automated red-teaming systems that generate, execute, and evaluate adversarial or safety-relevant interactions with generative models and model-based applications. We study automated red teaming across large language models, vision-language models, text-to-image and text-to-video systems, retrieval-augmented applications, and tool-using agents. We organize the literature around a closed-loop pipeline that connects risk objectives, test-case generation, interaction and execution, outcome judgment, failure triage, and mitigation. Within this pipeline, we unify red-teaming strategies by their search space, optimization signal, access assumptions, interaction horizon, and modality. We then compare evaluation metrics, benchmarks, automated judges, human-in-the-loop protocols, and reporting practices, with particular attention to attack success, harm severity, coverage, transferability, cost, and over-refusal. Finally, we identify open problems in adaptive coverage, reliable evaluation, stateful long-horizon testing, cross-modal and multilingual transfer, durable remediation, and governed disclosure. The survey is intended to provide a common vocabulary and a practical roadmap for building scalable, evidence-based automated red-teaming evaluations.



\end{abstract}
\keywords{automated red teaming; generative models; large language models; vision-language models; text-to-image generation; AI safety; adversarial evaluation; jailbreaks}

\subsection{Learning from Red-Team Failures}

Early automated red teaming focused primarily on scalable failure discovery and characterization~\cite{ganguli2022redteaming,perez2022redteaming}. Subsequent work uses red-team outputs directly as training signals. Verified failure traces from red teaming provide adversarial prompts for generating safe demonstrations, supply negative anchors for preference optimization, identify hazardous concepts for unlearning, and update attack sets as targets evolve~\cite{bianchi2024safetytuned,ge2024mart,lee2025diverseattacks,zhao2025diverct,mazeika2024harmbench,wu2025trident}. This connects red teaming directly to defensive alignment, feeding discovered failures back into model development.

\noindent\textbf{Adversarially Derived Alignment and Preference Optimization.} The most direct way to use red-team findings for remediation is to pair adversarial instructions with safe responses and fine-tune the target model on these curated demonstrations. For example, MART~\cite{ge2024mart} and TRIDENT~\cite{wu2025trident} generate adversarial prompts and synthesize corresponding safe completions for supervised fine-tuning, giving the model concrete examples of safe behavior on inputs it previously failed. Beyond supervised demonstrations, red-team traces supply critical negative completions for preference modeling. When an automated attacker elicits an unsafe response, the resulting trajectory provides an empirical negative example $y_{\text{harm}}$ paired with an aligned alternative $y_{\text{safe}}$, directly yielding preference pairs for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)~\cite{rafailov2023directpreference} or Safe RLHF~\cite{dai2024saferlhf,ji2023beavertails}. This grounds the optimization signal in real vulnerabilities rather than synthetic approximations. Furthermore, targeted representation editing and unlearning methods use failure activations to selectively erase hazardous domain knowledge while preserving general task performance~\cite{yao2024unlearning}.

\noindent\textbf{Coverage-Oriented Data Design.} The success of safety tuning depends on how well the training distribution covers distinct attack vectors without degrading benign utility. Lee et~al.~\cite{lee2025diverseattacks} train red-team policies to produce prompts with varied behavioral profiles, using the resulting examples for defensive tuning. DiveR-CT~\cite{zhao2025diverct} relaxes generation constraints to broaden the attack space, while TRIDENT~\cite{wu2025trident} structures data generation across lexical variations, malicious intent categories, and jailbreak tactics. Crucially, defensive data curation must guard against over-refusal: penalizing sensitive keywords indiscriminately can lead models to reject benign requests. Incorporating contrastive evaluation sets, such as XSTest~\cite{rottger2024xstest} and OR-Bench~\cite{cui2025orbench}, helps calibrate the defensive data distribution so that refusal behaviors align with harmful intent rather than surface vocabulary~\cite{pan2025overrefusal}.

\noindent\textbf{Dynamic Adversarial Training.} Fine-tuning changes model behavior, altering the vulnerabilities that red teams need to find. MART~\cite{ge2024mart} alternates between attack generation and safety fine-tuning across multiple rounds, keeping new attacks focused on the latest model state. Similarly, R2D2~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench} maintains a pool of adversarial test cases that are optimized continuously as the model updates. A central empirical question in dynamic training is whether defensive updates generalize to unseen attack strategies or merely memorize specific prompt templates. Evaluating remediation against held-out harm categories and cross-model transfer attacks ensures that safety gains reflect genuine robustness rather than surface-level pattern memorization.

While training-time remediation updates model weights, deployed systems must still handle untrusted inputs and dynamic context in production. Section~7.2 examines runtime defenses that inspect incoming prompts, guide decoding, and moderate generated outputs without modifying the underlying model.

\subsection{Turning Safety Judgments into Runtime Control}

Even after training-time remediation, deployed models remain vulnerable to adaptive attacks and previously unseen exploit variants~\cite{andriushchenko2025adaptive,andriushchenko2025pasttense}. Inference-time mitigation provides complementary defense by applying runtime interventions without altering model weights. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:section7_flowmap} and categorized in Table~\ref{tab:runtime_defenses}, these interventions operate across three distinct stages: pre-generation input filtering, in-generation decoding control, and post-generation output moderation.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Input Guard.} Input guards examine or sanitize incoming prompts and retrieved context prior to model inference. Dedicated guard models, such as Llama Guard~\cite{inan2023llamaguard}, ShieldGemma~\cite{zeng2024shieldgemma}, and WildGuard~\cite{han2024wildguard}, evaluate prompts and outputs against predefined safety taxonomies to detect harmful requests, jailbreak attempts, and inappropriate content. To defend against indirect prompt injection, BIPIA~\cite{yi2025bipia} introduces boundary-aware representations and explicit reminder mechanisms, whereas Spotlighting~\cite{hines2024spotlighting} incorporates provenance encodings to demarcate untrusted external data from system instructions. Robustness-oriented defenses mitigate sensitivity to adversarial prompt modifications: SmoothLLM~\cite{robey2023smoothllm} generates randomized character- and token-level perturbations of an input and aggregates predictions across copies, whereas Erase-and-Check~\cite{kumar2023erasecheck} evaluates token-subsequences to provide certified robustness guarantees against adversarial token insertions. SemanticSmooth~\cite{ji2024semanticsmoothing} generalizes randomized smoothing to semantic-preserving paraphrases. Collectively, these approaches broaden input defense from isolated keyword or intent filtering to structural context integrity and certified adversarial robustness.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Decoding Control.} Decoding interventions steer the token generation process dynamically, avoiding the need to rely solely on pre- or post-generation filtering. SafeDecoding~\cite{xu2024safedecoding} modulates token probability distributions using safety-guided directional vectors during generation, whereas Reasoning-to-Defend~\cite{zhu2025reasoningtodefend} enforces an intermediate safety-deliberation step prior to emitting the final response. Jailbreak Antidote~\cite{shen2025jailbreakantidote} guides hidden representations at inference time to suppress the activation of harmful concepts. Although these strategies preserve the underlying model parameters, they frequently incur additional computational overhead, increased response latency, and delicate trade-offs between safety and general utility.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Output Moderation.} Output moderation evaluates, filters, or rewrites candidate responses prior to returning them to the user. LLM Self Defense~\cite{phute2023llmselfdefense} prompts the model to self-audit its generated text for safety violations, whereas Backtranslation~\cite{wang2024backtranslation} infers the underlying user intent from the generated response to verify compliance. AutoDefense~\cite{zeng2024autodefense} distributes output screening across specialized, cooperating defender agents. AutoJailbreak~\cite{lu2024autojailbreak} provides a modular framework to analyze and coordinate dependencies across attack, defense, and evaluation pipelines, demonstrating how input- and output-stage defenses can be composed synergistically. While post-generation moderation introduces a critical layer of defense, its effectiveness remains bounded by the accuracy of the verification model and offers no recourse if an unsafe generation has already triggered external tool execution.

While these inference-time defenses establish multi-stage protection for text-to-text interactions, they do not inherently restrict tool execution authority, safeguard persistent memory, or isolate external side effects. Addressing these vulnerabilities demands system-level safeguards---including information-flow boundaries, capability-based permissions, action validation, execution containment, and transaction rollback---which are examined in Section~7.3.

\subsection{System-Level Protection Beyond Model Alignment}

As LLMs evolve from standalone chatbots into integrated systems with tools, memory, APIs, and autonomous workflows, the relevant security perimeter extends far beyond the parameter space of the model. Studies spanning indirect prompt injection, tool-augmented environments, agent memory, and agent security benchmarks demonstrate that adversaries can hijack execution via untrusted user inputs, adversarial instructions embedded in retrieved external data, poisoned memory stores, and subverted control paths~\cite{perez2022promptinject,abdelnabi2023indirect,debenedetti2024agentdojo,chen2024agentpoison,zhang2025asb}. Furthermore, safety guarantees frequently deteriorate following downstream fine-tuning of open-weight models~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,yang2023shadowalignment}. Consequently, parameter alignment alone cannot ensure instruction integrity, enforce authorization policies, or protect persistent system state. Robust defense requires system-level architectures that regulate information flow, constrain execution privileges, and mitigate the fallout of unsafe actions.

Table~\ref{tab:system_protection_capabilities} categorizes representative defense mechanisms by architectural placement rather than empirical effectiveness. The majority of existing defenses focus on pre-execution prevention. Post-execution recovery mechanisms remain rare: while early system defenses focused almost exclusively on intercepting operations before execution, recent architectures such as Cordon~\cite{chen2026cordon}, SafeHarness~\cite{lin2026safeharness}, and AIR~\cite{xiao2026air} establish state recovery and containment through transactional rollbacks, adaptive service degradation, or dynamic incident remediation. The overall trajectory reflects an architectural progression: beginning with information-flow separation, advancing to fine-grained authorization and action validation, and culminating in execution containment and reversible state recovery.

\noindent\textbf{Context and Information-Flow Control.} A secure system must prevent untrusted external inputs from subverting user intent or escalating execution authority. Perez and Ribeiro~\cite{perez2022promptinject} characterize goal hijacking and system prompt exfiltration through adversarial prompt injection. Greshake et~al.~\cite{abdelnabi2023indirect} formalize indirect prompt injection in application environments, demonstrating that adversarial payloads embedded within web pages or emails can hijack an agent prior to tool invocation or private data retrieval. Chen et~al.~\cite{chen2024agentpoison} demonstrate with AgentPoison that adversaries can corrupt the long-term memory or knowledge base of an agent, persistently compromising future trajectories. These threats reveal two fundamental security boundaries: ephemeral context assembled for the active task and durable state retained across sessions. StruQ~\cite{chen2025struq} enforces structural separation between instructions and data at the query interface, whereas Wu et~al.~\cite{wu2024fsecure} cast defense against indirect prompt injection into a formal information-flow control problem in the $f$-secure architecture. CaMeL~\cite{debenedetti2025camel} extends this principle to dynamic execution, isolating trusted control flows from untrusted inputs and enforcing capability-based access control on their interactions. Similarly, IPIGuard~\cite{an-etal-2025-ipiguard} binds the agent to a predetermined tool-dependency graph prior to ingesting external data, preventing injected instructions from altering the planned execution topology. Robust system architectures must therefore track data provenance and trust levels across retrieval, memory, planning, and tool execution, strictly circumscribing the privileges granted to untrusted content.

\noindent\textbf{Authorization and Action Validation.} Independent application-level monitors restrict executable actions following intent interpretation. Progent~\cite{shi2025progent} introduces programmable privilege policies, whereas MiniScope~\cite{zhu2025miniscope} enforces least-privilege scoping across tool invocations. Task Shield~\cite{jia2025taskshield} verifies whether proposed tool actions remain faithful to the original task assigned by the user. AuthBench~\cite{do2026leastprivilege} reveals that coding agents frequently request redundant or sensitive privileges, a tendency that persists even when augmented with extended reasoning. These frameworks fundamentally decouple model proposals from application authorizations: while the model may suggest an operation, an external reference monitor decides whether the execution is permissible based on user-defined constraints, granted capabilities, and the global state of the system.

\noindent\textbf{Runtime Monitoring and Intervention.} While static checks validate discrete actions in isolation, continuous runtime monitoring verifies that multi-step execution traces remain strictly within policy boundaries. Comprehensive evaluation incorporates tool arguments, execution outputs, prior action sequences, active privilege levels, and environmental state changes. ToolSafe~\cite{mou2026toolsafe} audits individual tool invocations against the interaction history, supplying real-time guardrail feedback prior to dispatch. MELON~\cite{pmlr-v267-zhu25z} re-executes agent trajectories with the user prompt masked; behavioral congruence between the original and masked executions serves as evidence that injected instructions---rather than legitimate user goals---are driving the system. GuardAgent~\cite{pmlr-v267-xiang25a} translates natural-language safety specifications into executable verification code to monitor agent actions, while ShieldAgent~\cite{pmlr-v267-chen25ae} reasons over action trajectories against formal policy specifications to verify safety plans. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate that runtime surveillance can operate at the granularity of individual invocations, historical traces, or formalized logic. Nonetheless, they remain predominantly preventive, focusing on intercepting operations immediately before an unsafe side effect is realized.

\noindent\textbf{Containment and Recovery.} Preventive detection alone cannot resolve failures once an action modifies environmental state or triggers irreversible side effects. IsolateGPT~\cite{wu2025isolategpt} establishes execution sandboxes that confine untrusted application components from the broader operating environment. To provide reversible execution, Cordon~\cite{chen2026cordon} introduces task-scoped transactional semantics: internal state updates are buffered in shadow memory, external actions are staged, and full multi-step executions are validated atomically prior to commit, enabling clean aborts and rollbacks upon policy violation. Extending this lifecycle perspective, SafeHarness~\cite{lin2026safeharness} couples runtime state validation with automated rollbacks and adaptive service degradation, while AIR~\cite{xiao2026air} pairs contextual incident detection with dynamic containment, environment recovery, and automated rule synthesis. Furthermore, benchmarks such as AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, ASB~\cite{zhang2025asb}, and ToolEmu~\cite{ruan2024toolemu} provide standardized testbeds to rigorously stress-test these mechanisms, although benchmark harnesses must not be conflated with production defenses. Overall, state recovery and fault containment remain considerably less explored than preventive filtering, highlighting an urgent need for resilient system designs.

\subsection{Continuous Testing After Remediation}

Empirical safety guarantees remain intrinsically coupled to the specific model weights, software dependencies, and runtime configurations under which vulnerabilities were initially assessed. Downstream fine-tuning can severely degrade safety alignment, even when adaptation corpora are strictly benign~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,peng2026cwac}; safety degradation varies widely across model architectures and evaluation protocols~\cite{khan2026safetydrift}; and formatting differences across prompt templates can silently disrupt aligned refusal behaviors~\cite{lyu2024keepingaligned}. These dynamics necessitate continuous red-teaming pipelines that archive historical failures for regression testing while systematically probing targets as they undergo ongoing refinement.

\noindent\textbf{Failure Retention.} Reusing red-team findings requires preserving comprehensive provenance to reproduce the observed failure. Drawing from vulnerability bounty programs, McGregor~\cite{mcgregor2025toerr} emphasizes structured review workflows and concrete audit artifacts required to validate flaws. Paeth et~al.~\cite{paeth2025lessons} demonstrate that structured incident databases facilitate longitudinal analysis and retrospective auditing when the root causes or broader impacts of failures remain ambiguous. Furthermore, FLARE-AI~\cite{longpre2026flareai} identifies system metadata, deterministic reproduction traces, and empirical evidence as foundational reporting dimensions, utilizing machine-readable schemas to streamline triage and cross-team coordination. Collectively, these practices demonstrate that failure retention must bind the original attack trace to the exact target version, the observed safety breakdown, and the complete environmental configuration necessary for deterministic replay.

\noindent\textbf{Safety Drift.} Safety evaluations conducted on a base model do not transfer unconditionally to downstream adapted variants. Qi et~al.~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels} demonstrate that fine-tuning on even entirely benign data can compromise previously aligned safeguards. Lyu et~al.~\cite{lyu2024keepingaligned} establish that formatting templates used during tuning and inference substantially govern alignment retention, demonstrating that prompt wrappers constitute a confounding variable in longitudinal safety comparisons. Khan et~al.~\cite{khan2026safetydrift} analyze this phenomenon across more than one hundred model variants, revealing pronounced and heterogeneous safety drift where improvements on one benchmark frequently coincide with regressions on another. Addressing this fragility, Peng et~al.~\cite{peng2026cwac} show that benign updates erode learned refusal boundaries, motivating explicit weight- and activation-space constraints during adaptation. Consequently, continuous red teaming must track safety deltas across successive model versions through rigorous regression testing, rather than presuming that historical safety guarantees persist across updates.

\noindent\textbf{Continuous Testing.} Regression replay and renewed exploit discovery form two complementary pillars for sustaining safety assurance across deployment cycles. EvoJail~\cite{tang2026evojail} evolves attack generation dynamically to match updating target versions, whereas AutoRedTeamer~\cite{zhou2025autoredteamer} continuously assimilates newly emerging exploitation strategies into a growing test suite. Similarly, Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} distills candidate jailbreaks into modular, easily maintainable safety benchmarks, and AgenticEval~\cite{wang2026agenticeval} develops self-updating evaluation regimens for agentic systems. Synthesizing these methodologies, continuous red teaming must integrate regression testing against archived vulnerabilities with active, exploratory red teaming against the updated system. In production settings, achieving this parity demands deterministic execution harnesses, immutable audit logging, and reusable evaluation workflows capable of operating robustly across evolving model architectures and environment configurations.

\noindent\textbf{Harness Capabilities.} An evaluation harness turns a red-teaming strategy into an executable experiment by coordinating five core functions. First, target adapters connect to model APIs or application endpoints through a unified interface. Second, attack orchestration manages prompt selection, seed generation, query budgets, and interaction schedules. Third, automated judges evaluate model outputs, tool invocations, or terminal system states to generate structured verdicts. Fourth, execution traces record the inputs, outputs, runtime parameters, software versions, and intermediate scores behind those decisions. Finally, replay mechanisms re-execute test cases under original conditions or against updated targets, judges, and environments to verify findings and detect regressions. In Table~\ref{tab:redteam_harnesses}, Generate and Adaptive describe test-case synthesis and search. Judge and Multimodal specify supported interaction modalities and evaluation mechanisms, while Record and Replay indicate whether experimental evidence is preserved and reusable. An entry indicating support for Judge reflects an integrated scoring interface rather than guaranteed label accuracy.

\noindent\textbf{Representative Designs.} Existing harnesses reflect different operational trade-offs across these capabilities. For vulnerability scanning, garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} pairs security probes and generators with dedicated detectors in a plugin architecture. PyRIT~\cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit} coordinates targets, orchestrators, prompt converters, and scorers into reusable workflows that support multi-turn and multimodal red teaming. For application testing, promptfoo~\cite{promptfooDocs} offers a declarative framework to configure model providers, attack plugins, and automated assertions. Inspect AI~\cite{ukaisi2024inspect} couples model interfaces with tools and containerized sandboxes, retaining detailed logs for retrospective auditing and re-scoring. These design differences stem from distinct testing goals. A simple campaign may only need single-turn prompt probes, whereas testing tool-using agents requires recording multi-step state transitions across external environments. In both cases, the harness establishes the experimental boundary and provenance records needed to make empirical safety claims verifiable.

\noindent\textbf{Attack Engines and Evaluation Suites.} Attack-generation frameworks such as GPTFuzzer~\cite{yu2023gptfuzzer}, APRT~\cite{jiang2024aprt}, AutoDAN~\cite{liu2024autodan}, and TAP~\cite{mehrotra2024tap} focus on synthesizing adversarial prompts and optimizing search. In the taxonomy of Table~\ref{tab:redteam_harnesses}, they serve as workload generators rather than general execution platforms. In contrast, evaluation suites and benchmark environments fix part of the protocol to enable controlled comparisons. For instance, HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench} and JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench} establish standardized harm categories, baseline attacks, and automated evaluation routines. Similarly, AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} provides a simulated environment to test tool-using agents against prompt injection, and MM-SafetyBench~\cite{liu2024mmsafetybench} extends standardized safety benchmarks to multimodal models. This distinction clarifies how benchmarks relate to harnesses: benchmarks supply test inputs, threat models, and evaluation criteria, while harnesses provide the underlying software environment to run, record, and replay those tests.

\noindent\textbf{Static and Renewable Workloads.} Static benchmark suites remain useful because fixed test cases and scoring rubrics allow consistent comparisons across models and defenses. However, their coverage is restricted to predefined categories and templates, which risk becoming obsolete as defense mechanisms and attack strategies change. Addressing this limitation, h4rm3l~\cite{doumbouya2025h4rm3l} models adversarial jailbreaks as composable syntactic and semantic transformations, generating novel attacks that expose blind spots in static datasets. Similarly, Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} extracts varied failure traces into modular, updateable benchmarks. Static suites, renewable workloads, and harnesses therefore serve complementary roles. Static suites provide stable baselines, renewable workloads refresh test content against updating models, and harnesses preserve the execution configurations and traces needed to run both over time. Even so, automated tools cannot decide which risks matter in a given deployment or resolve culturally ambiguous outputs. Those foundational tasks require human judgment, as discussed in the next subsection.

\subsection{Human Guidance and Automation in Red Teaming}

\noindent\textbf{Defining the Standard.} Automated red teaming requires an explicit operational standard that defines the goals of a campaign. This standard specifies which risks belong in scope, which system boundaries are evaluated, what constitutes harmful behavior or an invalid action, and what evidence is required to confirm a safety failure. These choices determine both the vulnerabilities that can be found and how reported results should be interpreted. STAR~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star} structures human involvement through parameterized instructions for red teamers, demographic matching between annotators and harm topics, and formal rules to resolve divergent judgments. In addition, an empirical study by Alvarado Garcia et al.~\cite{alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical} shows that choices made by practitioners regarding risk taxonomies, deployment scenarios, dataset generation, and evaluation rubrics directly determine which user populations and cultural contexts are represented in red-teaming benchmarks.

\noindent\textbf{Revising the Standard.} Evaluation standards must also reflect the contexts where models will be deployed. Adversarial Nibbler~\cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler} uses crowdsourced exploration to gather implicit adversarial prompts, identifying text-to-image safety failures that automated pipelines often miss. Going PLACES~\cite{rastogi2026goingplaces} works with local communities to incorporate regional cultural and linguistic norms into evaluation cases. Such human-centered efforts update the risk register rather than merely growing static benchmarks. They reveal unmodeled harm categories, overlooked system interfaces, and failure modes that cannot be diagnosed from isolated text outputs alone. Furthermore, DICES~\cite{NEURIPS2023_a74b697b} and D3CODE~\cite{mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code} document widespread disagreement in safety and offensiveness ratings across different demographics and cultures. Such disagreement provides a valuable signal that an evaluation rule needs explicit human review, rather than being collapsed into an oversimplified majority label.

\noindent\textbf{Automation as a Multiplier.} Automation helps scale these human-defined standards. For example, AART~\cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart} uses modular evaluation recipes to define application-specific risk concepts and generate large adversarial test sets. Bridging the Scale Gap~\cite{quaye2026bridging} starts from human-written adversarial prompts and systematically expands them using sociolinguistic transformations learned from expert red teamers. Evaluation harnesses then execute these expanded test suites and record complete interaction traces for comparison and replay~\cite{derczynski2024garak,lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect,promptfooDocs}. Although automated judges can screen large batches of responses, their outputs depend entirely on the specified rubric, observable context, and decision threshold. High-stakes or ambiguous cases still require human audit and adjudication~\cite{souly2024strongreject,han2024wildguard,thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier}. Human oversight is therefore a regular operational requirement rather than an occasional exception. Experts must set evaluation criteria, analyze boundary failures and evaluative disagreements, and update risk registers, escalation thresholds, and disclosure rules as new evidence appears.

\subsection{Balancing Transparency and Exploitability}

Automated red teaming produces sensitive artifacts, including jailbreak prompts, multi-step exploit chains, attack scripts, and test cases, alongside empirical safety findings about a target system. Responsible disclosure therefore raises two governance challenges: deciding which artifacts can be shared with specific audiences, and determining what documentation is necessary to ensure findings remain auditable. Solaiman et al.~\cite{solaiman2019release} frame model and artifact release as a staged process rather than an all-or-nothing decision. An analysis of external red teaming from OpenAI~\cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming} shows that access controls and documentation standards are campaign-level governance decisions. In an offensive cybersecurity setting, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} demonstrate this balance by withholding full prompts and step-by-step reproduction instructions from the public, while granting access to qualified researchers upon request.

Vulnerability reporting and cross-organization coordination address a complementary question: how findings can be examined, fixed, and reported without creating unnecessary security risks. Cattell et al.~\cite{cattell2024coordinatedflaw} treat independent verification and formal review as key parts of coordinated flaw disclosure. Similarly, Householder et al.~\cite{householder2024lessons} outline an end-to-end disclosure lifecycle for machine learning vulnerabilities, covering discovery, validation, coordinated remediation, and public release. \noindent\textbf{Access Control.} The artifact-handling practices in Table~\ref{tab:redteam_disclosure} do not follow a rigid release hierarchy. Instead, they reflect three practical choices regarding artifact sensitivity, recipient scope, and public disclosure. First, regarding artifact sensitivity, benchmarks and fuzzers such as HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench}, JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}, and GPTFuzzer~\cite{yu2023gptfuzzer} release code and test cases openly to support public scrutiny. By contrast, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} withheld full prompts and reproduction steps to avoid lowering the barrier to misuse. Second, recipient scope can be restricted by vetting users. For example, Ahmad et al.~\cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming} describe tiered access for vetted researchers, while Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} share sensitive artifacts on request. Finally, public accounts can communicate safety findings responsibly without releasing actionable exploits, as illustrated by the high-level summaries in the GPT-4 system card~\cite{openai2023gpt4systemcard}. These examples show the value of documenting sensitivity assessments, access tiers, and release decisions for each artifact, even though universal disclosure rules remain an open problem.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting Standard.} Finding accountability is an independent requirement alongside artifact access control. A safety report must allow external auditors to evaluate what was tested, what evidence supports the finding, and how the vulnerability was handled. Complete reporting should capture the threat model and target configuration (including system versions, dependencies, and assumed attacker access), the evaluation procedure (including query budgets, judges, and success criteria), the availability of evidence and artifacts (distinguishing public, gated, and withheld materials), and the disclosure lifecycle (including validation, developer notification, mitigation, and publication timelines). Studies of bug bounties, such as McGregor et al.~\cite{mcgregor2025toerr}, highlight the importance of formal review workflows and supporting artifacts. Paeth et al.~\cite{paeth2025lessons} demonstrate that structured incident databases are essential for longitudinal search and analysis, while FLARE-AI~\cite{longpre2026flareai} provides a machine-readable schema for coordinated reporting. Together, these frameworks show what information should be preserved, though establishing a uniform reporting standard for automated red teaming remains an open challenge.

\section{Open Problems and Future Directions}
\label{sec:agenda}

The main challenge for automated red teaming is no longer just finding more successful attacks. A practical red-teaming system must connect tasks that are often evaluated in isolation: identifying meaningful risks, finding failures under realistic configurations, confirming that observed outcomes represent genuine vulnerabilities, and verifying that a defense reduces risk without harming benign performance. Earlier surveys organize the area around operational pipelines or around attack taxonomies and search strategies~\cite{purpura-etal-2025-building,Lin_2025}. Building on these perspectives, this survey treats automated red teaming as a closed loop. The research problems below address the missing pieces needed to make that loop dependable: adaptive coverage, reliable evaluation, stateful long-horizon testing, cross-modal and multilingual transfer, durable remediation, and governed disclosure. Figure~\ref{fig:section9_research_map} synthesizes these foundational challenges into an overarching research map organized across three core pillars: threat discovery, system execution and evaluation fidelity, and defensive durability and community governance.

\subsection{Adaptive Risk Coverage Beyond Prompt Counts}

\noindent\textbf{Mechanism-Level vs. Lexical Coverage.} Risk coverage must be measured at the level of failure mechanisms, not prompt counts. Automated methods can generate far more test cases than manual red teams, but the total number of prompts, attempts, or jailbreaks does not reflect true risk coverage. A large prompt collection often consists mostly of rephrased inputs that exploit the same underlying weakness. In contrast, a small set of cases can uncover distinct failure mechanisms, such as instruction hijacking through retrieved context, unauthorized tool calls, or persistent memory poisoning. Static benchmarks make controlled comparisons possible, but their fixed categories limit the risks they can surface~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. Red teaming therefore requires coverage metrics that distinguish superficial rewording from distinct failure mechanisms.

\noindent\textbf{Dynamic Risk Registers and Clustering.} One promising direction is to maintain a risk register during red-team search. Each confirmed failure can be recorded alongside its prompt, outcome, failure category, system boundary, interaction context, and reproduction steps. Novelty and clustering metrics can then be assessed by whether they separate cases with distinct root causes, instead of measuring superficial linguistic variation. Quality-diversity search and diversity-oriented red teaming provide useful initial frameworks~\cite{zhao2025diverct,li2024rtpe,jiang2024aprt,wu2025trident,zhou2025autoredteamer}. The main open problem is deciding whether a newly found case reflects a new failure mechanism or simply a familiar attack in different words.

\noindent\textbf{Participatory Risk Space Definition.} Coverage also depends on who defines the risk space. End users, domain experts, and community members frequently identify harms and deployment contexts that do not appear in fixed safety taxonomies~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,quaye2024adversarialnibbler,rastogi2026goingplaces}. Participatory red teaming provides a path for including these perspectives, but automated tools must preserve the context that made a human finding meaningful when generating variations~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,quaye2026bridging}. Future evaluations should report both which risks were tested and where those risk definitions originated. This framing treats coverage as an explicit record of what was tested, rather than an implicit claim that all relevant risks have been examined.

\subsection{Decision-Grade Evaluation and Trustworthy Judges}

\noindent\textbf{The Judge Calibration and Hacking Dilemma.} A reported attack is only as credible as the evaluation protocol used to judge it. The value of a discovered failure depends on the accuracy of the judge that labels it. Automated judges enable large-scale evaluation, but different classifiers, heuristic rules, and LLM evaluators often assign conflicting labels to the same response. Disagreements with human annotators are common in ambiguous, conversational, or culturally dependent settings~\cite{souly2024strongreject,han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,zheng2023mtbench,chen2024humansjudge,thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier,eiras2025knowthyjudge,NEURIPS2023_a74b697b,mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code}. Adversarial search methods can also exploit weaknesses in the judge itself, producing outputs that fool the evaluator rather than exposing a true system failure~\cite{raina2024robustjudge,tong2025badjudge,shi2024optimization}. An attack success rate is therefore not an absolute property of a model, but depends on the evaluator, rubric, and decision threshold that produced it.

\noindent\textbf{Treating the Protocol as an Evaluand.} Future work should treat the evaluation protocol as part of the system under test. For any safety claim, studies should clearly define what counts as a failure, whether harmful text, unauthorized actions, privacy leaks, policy violations, or task disruption. Authors should document what evidence the judge can observe, together with the judge version, prompt or rubric, and decision threshold. While human review cannot cover every generated interaction, it remains essential for calibrating automated judges on high-stakes and ambiguous cases. Instead of reporting only a single summary agreement score, studies should analyze where automated judges and human raters disagree.

\noindent\textbf{Component-Specific Fault Attribution.} This challenge grows more difficult in agentic and multimodal \mbox{systems}. A text-only judge might label an intermediate response as safe even when the surrounding trajectory leads to an unsafe tool call. Conversely, looking only at the final outcome can hide whether the failure came from the model, a tool, a permission setting, or the environment. Evaluation must therefore link the failure criterion directly to the system component being tested. The goal is not to build a universal safety judge, but to establish clear protocols that show which conclusions are supported by evidence and where uncertainty remains.

\subsection{Stateful and Long-Horizon System Testing}

\noindent\textbf{The Trajectory as the Core Testing Unit.} In complex applications, the proper unit of testing is an interaction trajectory rather than an isolated response. Most red-teaming evaluations focus on single prompt-response pairs. This setup breaks down when a model retrieves external documents, maintains memory, calls tools, or takes actions across changing environments. In these settings, an early step that looks harmless can lead to an unsafe outcome later, and the failure is often an unauthorized action or an irreversible real-world effect rather than toxic text. Benchmarks for agents have started to capture these behaviors, including indirect prompt injection, tool-based privilege escalation, and multi-step task failures~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhan2024injecagent,ruan2024toolemu,chen2024agentpoison,zou2025poisonedrag,wang2025webinject}.

\noindent\textbf{Structured Trajectory Logging.} The next step is to evaluate trajectories as structured objects. A test record should capture the initial task, retrieved context, memory state, active permissions, tool calls, intermediate decisions, and final outcomes. This detail makes it possible to determine how a defense works: whether it blocks an unsafe action, changes how the model interprets untrusted content, or simply causes the task to fail early. It also supports evaluations of containment and recovery, which are impossible to assess when looking only at a final text label.

\noindent\textbf{Compounding Errors and Environment Simulation.} Persistent interactions also introduce compounding failure modes that single-turn harnesses cannot diagnose. Over extended horizons, subtle errors in tool output parsing, conversational state drift, and nondeterministic environment feedback can accumulate, steering agents into unauthorized operational states. Developing evaluation regimens for these multi-step workflows requires simulated environments that support deterministic state checkpointing, reversible action mocking, and fine-grained trajectory attribution. Without structured tracing across the full interaction lifecycle, red teaming cannot distinguish whether an operational failure stems from model reasoning errors, tool interface ambiguities, or brittle system orchestration.

\subsection{Cross-Modal, Multilingual, and Systemic Transfer}

\noindent\textbf{Modal and Linguistic Attack Surface Expansion.} Adversarial vulnerabilities and defenses do not transfer uniformly across modalities and languages. While prompt injection and jailbreaks have been extensively characterized in text-centric English benchmarks, multimodal systems expand the attack surface through visual prompt injections, typographic exploits, and cross-modal embedding conflicts~\cite{liu2024mmsafetybench,ying2024safebench,liu2025videosafetybench}. Similarly, safety alignment frequently degrades across low-resource languages, dialects, and code-switching scenarios, where safety guardrails exhibit pronounced blind spots compared to English baselines~\cite{deng2024multilingual,yong2023lowresource,rastogi2026goingplaces}. Automated red teaming must therefore move beyond monolingual text probes to systematically test failure transferability across vision, audio, video, and diverse linguistic contexts.

\noindent\textbf{Formalizing the Four-Axis Transfer Matrix.} Attack transferability should also be treated as a system-level property rather than a feature of the attack alone. Formally, we define the evaluation space over four constituent dimensions $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{M} \times \mathcal{D} \times \mathcal{E}$, spanning attack representations $a \in \mathcal{A}$, target foundation models $m \in \mathcal{M}$, defense alignment strategies $d \in \mathcal{D}$, and host deployment runtimes $e \in \mathcal{E}$. The empirical transferability $\mathcal{T}(a, m, d, e) \in [0, 1]$ measures adversarial success under a specific configuration. An attack may transfer readily across base models ($m \to m'$) yet fail when wrapped in a specialized retrieval pipeline or system prompt ($e \to e'$). Conversely, architectural flaws in tool orchestration may recur across diverse base models that share identical control flow. Evaluating $\mathcal{T}$ across this four-axis matrix systematically isolates vulnerabilities caused by the model from weaknesses introduced by the host application~\cite{liu2023houyi,wu2025darkfunction}, providing actionable diagnostic resolution beyond scalar source-to-target transfer rates.

\noindent\textbf{Decoupling Model Weights from Host Runtimes.} Decoupling model vulnerabilities from application integration flaws is essential for actionable remediation. When an attack succeeds against an enterprise assistant, the root cause may lie in permissive tool authorization, unvetted retrieval embeddings, or underlying foundation model misbehavior. By quantifying transferability across modular pipelines, red-team evaluations can pinpoint whether a discovered flaw requires model weight retraining, runtime input sanitization, or architectural privilege scoping. Establishing standardized cross-system transfer benchmarks will allow practitioners to assess whether mitigations generalize across deployment targets or remain fragile to runtime reconfiguration.

\subsection{Durable Safety Improvement Under Changing Conditions}

\noindent\textbf{Beyond Static Replay.} Safety improvements must remain meaningful as models and evaluation conditions evolve. Closing the evaluation loop requires more than replaying old attacks against new checkpoints. A fix can easily suppress known prompts without addressing the underlying failure mechanism, reduce one type of harm while increasing over-refusal on benign requests, or introduce new weaknesses in other application settings. Iterative red teaming and safety fine-tuning help address this issue by generating new attacks for updated models, while past failures provide regression benchmarks for tracking over time~\cite{zhou2025autoredteamer,zhang2025jbdistill}. The central challenge is confirming that an observed improvement reflects a real reduction in risk, rather than changes in the attacker, judge, or evaluation setup.

\noindent\textbf{Four-Factor Source Separation.} A continuous testing framework should separate four sources of change: the target system, the attack generator, the evaluation judge, and the test protocol. A change in any one of these can alter safety scores. Updates to the target can shift safety behavior after fine-tuning~\cite{khan2026safetydrift,peng2026cwac}. New attack suites can find failures missed by older tests~\cite{doumbouya2025h4rm3l,zhang2025jbdistill}. Updated judges can reclassify past outputs~\cite{thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier}. Versioned test traces should therefore store enough detail to replay interactions under original conditions and rerun them under updated rules. Comparing these runs clarifies whether a failure was fixed, changed in severity, or disappeared because the evaluation protocol changed.

\noindent\textbf{Holistic Defensive Cost Accounting.} Remediation studies should also evaluate practical costs alongside defensive gains. Demonstrating that a defense works requires identifying where the intervention occurs, testing against attack families not used during training, and reporting costs such as reduced performance on benign tasks, higher latency, human review overhead, or limited tool functionality. Confirmed failures should become permanent regression tests, but new discovery must continue alongside regression checks because replay only tests known weaknesses. This combination turns red teaming from a one-off demonstration into an ongoing process for measuring residual risk.

\subsection{Comparable Evidence and Governed Disclosure}

\noindent\textbf{Standardized Infrastructure for Comparable Evidence.} Safety evidence must be comparable across studies without making dangerous exploits widely accessible. Supporting these research directions requires shared infrastructure in addition to new attack algorithms. Standardized evaluation harnesses, renewable benchmarks, and structured incident reports preserve the configurations, interaction traces, judgments, and environments needed to compare results across systems and over time~\cite{ukaisi2024inspect,zhang2025jbdistill,mcgregor2025toerr,paeth2025lessons,longpre2026flareai}. Studies should report the threat model, system configuration, attacker access level, query budget, evaluator, success criteria, and artifact release policies. Without these details, differences between reported results often reflect incompatible test setups rather than true differences in robustness.

\noindent\textbf{Tiered Artifact Disclosure.} Scientific reproducibility does not require unrestricted release of every artifact. Some materials, such as aggregate metrics, report schemas, and evaluation code, can be released openly to support independent review. Other assets, including reliable jailbreaks, exploit chains, and automated attack scripts, can lower the barrier to misuse. Future work should establish tiered disclosure models that pair artifact sensitivity with appropriate access controls and minimum reporting requirements~\cite{solaiman2019release,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,cattell2024coordinatedflaw,householder2024lessons}. This approach allows external researchers to verify safety claims while limiting the spread of actionable exploit code through gated access or coordinated disclosure.

\noindent\textbf{Durable and Auditable Evidence as the Ultimate Goal.} The ultimate goal of automated red teaming is durable and auditable safety evidence, not simply a higher attack count. A mature system should document which risks were tested, how outcomes were judged, which system components failed, how vulnerabilities were addressed, and which evidence can be inspected independently. Meeting this standard requires care, but it is necessary if automated red teaming is to guide safety decisions rather than merely demonstrate isolated failures.
\section{Conclusion}

Automated red teaming should be evaluated as a closed-loop safety-evaluation system rather than as a collection of isolated jailbreak techniques. A useful survey must therefore connect risk specification, generation, search, interaction, judgment, coverage, mitigation, and responsible operational practice across generative-model targets.
