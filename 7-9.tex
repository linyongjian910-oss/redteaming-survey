\begin{abstract}
The rapid deployment of generative models has created a need for automated red-teaming methods that discover failures beyond manually authored test cases. This survey focuses on automated red-teaming systems that generate, execute, and evaluate adversarial or safety-relevant interactions with generative models and model-based applications. We study automated red teaming across large language models, vision-language models, text-to-image and text-to-video systems, retrieval-augmented applications, and tool-using agents. We organize the literature around a closed-loop pipeline that connects risk objectives, test-case generation, interaction and execution, outcome judgment, failure triage, and mitigation. Within this pipeline, we unify red-teaming strategies by their search space, optimization signal, access assumptions, interaction horizon, and modality. We then compare evaluation metrics, benchmarks, automated judges, human-in-the-loop protocols, and reporting practices, with particular attention to attack success, harm severity, coverage, transferability, cost, and over-refusal. Finally, we identify open problems in long-horizon interaction, multimodal and multilingual testing, reliable evaluation, adaptive coverage, reproducibility, and the responsible use of automated red-teaming systems. The survey is intended to provide a common vocabulary and a practical roadmap for building scalable, evidence-based automated red-teaming evaluations.



\end{abstract}

\subsection{Learning from Red-Team Failures}

Early automated red-teaming studies focused primarily on scalable failure discovery and characterization \cite{ganguli2022redteaming,perez2022redteaming}. More recent work treats discovered failures as training material: attacks can be converted into safe-response supervision, selected to broaden the training distribution, and regenerated as the target model changes \cite{ge2024mart,lee2025diverseattacks,zhao2025diverct,mazeika2024harmbench,wu2025trident}. Training-time remediation therefore forms a loop in which red teaming determines not only what is tested, but also part of what the model learns.

\noindent\textbf{Failure-Derived Supervision.} The most direct route from red-team evidence to remediation pairs a harmful or adversarial instruction with an aligned response and fine-tunes the target on those pairs. MART~\cite{ge2024mart} combines automatic adversarial prompt writing with safe-response generation, then updates the target model on the resulting safety-aligned data. TRIDENT~\cite{wu2025trident} similarly synthesizes harmful instructions and pairs them with ethically aligned responses for supervised fine-tuning. In both cases, a discovered or generated failure becomes a concrete supervision signal rather than only an evaluation result.

\noindent\textbf{Coverage-Oriented Data Design.} The quality of this supervision depends on which attack modes enter the training set. Learning Diverse Attacks~\cite{lee2025diverseattacks} trains red-team policies to generate effective prompts with diverse behaviors and uses the resulting cases for safety tuning. DiveR-CT~\cite{zhao2025diverct} relaxes generation constraints to enlarge the range of attacks used in downstream blue-team tuning. TRIDENT~\cite{wu2025trident} makes this coverage problem explicit by constructing data across lexical diversity, malicious intent, and jailbreak tactics. These approaches shift the emphasis from accumulating successful prompts to constructing a training distribution that represents different ways a model can be induced to fail.

\noindent\textbf{Dynamic Adversarial Training.} Training also changes the target that the red team must attack. MART~\cite{ge2024mart} alternates attack generation and safety fine-tuning across multiple rounds, so later prompts are constructed against the updated target rather than against an earlier model. R2D2~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench}, introduced in HarmBench, maintains a persistent pool of adversarial test cases that is continually optimized during training. The shared principle is to refresh adversarial pressure as the model changes, allowing later rounds to test residual weaknesses instead of only replaying the failures that produced the original update.

Training-time remediation changes model parameters using a constructed distribution of past and newly generated failures. Once a model is deployed, however, the system must still decide how to handle the particular prompts and external context that arrive at runtime. Section~7.2 therefore turns from training data to runtime controls that inspect inputs, guide decoding, and moderate completed outputs.

\subsection{Turning Safety Judgments into Runtime Control}

Training-time remediation cannot fully prevent failures caused by adaptive prompts and unseen attack strategies after deployment \cite{andriushchenko2025adaptive,andriushchenko2025pasttense}. Inference-time mitigation therefore introduces runtime interventions without modifying model parameters. As summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:section7_flowmap}, these interventions operate at three points: input guarding before generation, decoding control during generation, and output moderation after generation.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Input Guard.} Input guards inspect or transform prompts and retrieved context before the target model generates a response. Llama Guard~\cite{inan2023llamaguard}, ShieldGemma~\cite{zeng2024shieldgemma}, and WildGuard~\cite{han2024wildguard} provide reusable guard models for judging harmful requests, jailbreaks, refusals, and generated content. For indirect prompt injection, BIPIA~\cite{yi2025bipia} combines a benchmark with boundary-awareness and explicit-reminder defenses, while Spotlighting~\cite{hines2024spotlighting} adds provenance signals to distinguish external data from trusted instructions. Robustness-oriented defenses reduce sensitivity to adversarial prompt modifications: SmoothLLM~\cite{robey2023smoothllm} perturbs multiple copies of an input and aggregates their safety predictions, whereas Erase-and-Check~\cite{kumar2023erasecheck} applies a safety filter to token-erased subsequences and provides a bounded certificate against several insertion patterns. SemanticSmooth~\cite{ji2024semanticsmoothing} extends the smoothing idea to semantically transformed prompts. Together, these methods broaden input protection from direct harmful-request filtering to context integrity and adversarial-prompt robustness.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Decoding Control.} Decoding controls intervene while the response is being generated rather than filtering only the input or completed output. SafeDecoding~\cite{xu2024safedecoding} modifies token selection using safety-aware decoding signals, while Reasoning-to-Defend~\cite{zhu2025reasoningtodefend} inserts an explicit safety-reasoning stage before the final response. Jailbreak Antidote~\cite{shen2025jailbreakantidote} adjusts internal representations to reduce the likelihood of harmful generations. These approaches preserve the original model parameters, but may introduce additional computation, latency, or safety--utility trade-offs.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Output Moderation.} Output moderation acts after generation by evaluating, revising, or filtering model responses. LLM Self Defense~\cite{phute2023llmselfdefense} uses a response-side safety examination, while Backtranslation~\cite{wang2024backtranslation} reconstructs the underlying request intent to guide response correction. AutoDefense~\cite{zeng2024autodefense} extends this pattern through multiple defender agents that collaborate to filter harmful responses. AutoJailbreak~\cite{lu2024autojailbreak} is better understood as a framework for analyzing and composing dependencies among attack, defense, and evaluation methods; its AutoDefense component illustrates how pre-generative and post-generative defenses can be combined. These methods add a verification or correction stage after generation, but their effectiveness still depends on judge reliability and on whether harmful content has already produced an external effect.

The three forms of inference-time mitigation provide layered protection for model responses, but they do not by themselves constrain tool authority, persistent memory, or external side effects. Those broader controls require information-flow, permission, action-validation, containment, and recovery mechanisms, which are discussed as system-level protection in Section~7.3.

\subsection{System-Level Protection Beyond Model Alignment}

As LLMs evolve from standalone chatbots into integrated systems with tools, memory, APIs, and autonomous workflows, their security boundary expands beyond model parameters. Research on prompt injection, tool-integrated applications, agent memory, and agent-security benchmarks has found that adversaries can redirect a deployed system through malicious user inputs, instructions embedded in external content, poisoned persistent state, and compromised control paths \cite{perez2022promptinject,abdelnabi2023indirect,debenedetti2024agentdojo,chen2024agentpoison,zhang2025asb}. Safety behavior can also weaken after downstream fine-tuning of open-weight models \cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,yang2023shadowalignment}. Alignment therefore does not guarantee instruction integrity, authorized execution, or durable state integrity after deployment. System-level protection must control information flow, executable authority, and the consequences of agent actions.

The matrix separates the location of a defense boundary from its measured effectiveness. Most of the surveyed methods intervene before an external effect is committed. Within this comparison, Cordon is the only method that explicitly implements recovery through staged effects, commit, abort, and rollback. The pattern therefore supports a progression from information and authority separation, through authorization and action checks, toward execution containment and recovery.

\noindent\textbf{Context and Information-Flow Control.} A system must keep untrusted content from redefining the task or gaining authority over later actions. At the prompt layer, \emph{Ignore Previous Prompt}~\cite{perez2022promptinject} documents goal hijacking and prompt leakage through crafted inputs. Greshake et al.~\cite{abdelnabi2023indirect} extend the threat to LLM-integrated applications, where instructions hidden in webpages or emails can redirect an agent that reads external content before calling tools or handling private data. AgentPoison~\cite{chen2024agentpoison} shows that an attacker can poison an agent's memory or knowledge base and thereby influence later behavior. These attacks expose two integrity boundaries: the context assembled for the current task and the state retained for future tasks. StruQ~\cite{chen2025struq} separates instructions from data at the query interface, while the $f$-secure system~\cite{wu2024fsecure} frames indirect-prompt-injection defense as information-flow control. CaMeL~\cite{debenedetti2025camel} carries this separation into agent execution by distinguishing trusted control flow from untrusted data and using capabilities to restrict their interaction. IPIGuard~\cite{an-etal-2025-ipiguard} similarly commits the agent to a planned tool-dependency graph before it reads external data, so that newly injected instructions cannot redefine the execution path. A practical design should preserve the source and trust level of information through retrieval, memory, planning, and execution, then restrict the operations that untrusted content may trigger.

\noindent\textbf{Authorization and Action Validation.} Independent application controls limit what an agent can do after it interprets a request. Progent~\cite{shi2025progent} exposes programmable privilege policies, and MiniScope~\cite{zhu2025miniscope} applies least-privilege authorization to tool calls. Task Shield~\cite{jia2025taskshield} instead validates whether a proposed tool action remains aligned with the user's task. AuthBench~\cite{do2026leastprivilege} finds that coding agents may seek unnecessary or sensitive permissions, even when extra inference-time reasoning is available. These methods treat a model decision and an application authorization as separate events. The agent may propose an action, but an independent control must decide whether that action is permitted under the user's task, the available capabilities, and the current system state.

\noindent\textbf{Runtime Monitoring and Intervention.} A one-time action check evaluates an isolated proposal; runtime monitoring evaluates whether an evolving execution trace remains within the task and policy boundary. The relevant evidence can include tool arguments and results, the sequence of preceding actions, current privileges, and state changes caused by earlier calls. ToolSafe~\cite{mou2026toolsafe} monitors individual tool invocations against the interaction history and returns guardrail feedback before execution. MELON~\cite{pmlr-v267-zhu25z} instead re-executes an agent trajectory with the user prompt masked, treating similar actions in the original and masked runs as evidence that an injected instruction, rather than the user task, is controlling the agent. GuardAgent~\cite{pmlr-v267-xiang25a} converts a safety request into executable guardrail code that checks target-agent actions, while ShieldAgent~\cite{pmlr-v267-chen25ae} reasons over the action trajectory against explicit policy rules and formally verifies its shielding plan. Together, these methods show that runtime monitoring can operate at the level of a call, an action history, or an explicit policy, but they remain primarily preventive: they seek to intervene before the next unsafe effect is committed.

\noindent\textbf{Containment and Recovery.} Detection is insufficient once an action has altered system state or initiated an external effect. IsolateGPT~\cite{wu2025isolategpt} provides an execution-isolation boundary that limits how untrusted applications interact with the wider system. Cordon~\cite{chen2026cordon} introduces a task-scoped transactional boundary: reversible changes occur in shadow state, outward-facing effects are staged, and the composed execution is validated before commit, allowing abort and rollback when the task-level policy fails. Recent work broadens this lifecycle view. SafeHarness~\cite{lin2026safeharness} specifies safe rollback and adaptive degradation at state update, and AIR~\cite{xiao2026air} integrates incident detection from recent context and environment state with containment, recovery, and later rule synthesis. AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, ASB~\cite{zhang2025asb}, and ToolEmu~\cite{ruan2024toolemu} provide test environments for examining these controls without treating an evaluation environment itself as a runtime defense. Recovery mechanisms nevertheless remain far less established than preventive runtime controls.

\subsection{Continuous Testing After Remediation}

Red-team evidence is tied to the target model and execution configuration under which a failure was observed. Fine-tuning can weaken safety alignment even when the adaptation data are benign~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,peng2026cwac}; the resulting safety changes can vary across adapted models and evaluation instruments~\cite{khan2026safetydrift}; and prompt templates used during adaptation and inference can alter whether the aligned behavior is retained~\cite{lyu2024keepingaligned}. Continuous red teaming therefore requires both preserving past failures for later replay and reevaluating safety as the target evolves.

\noindent\textbf{Failure Retention.} Reusing a red-team failure requires preserving enough evidence to reconstruct the original evaluation. \emph{To Err Is AI}~\cite{mcgregor2025toerr} draws reporting practices from an open-ended bug bounty, emphasizing the review process and the artifacts used to substantiate flaws. \emph{Lessons for Editors of AI Incidents from the AI Incident Database}~\cite{paeth2025lessons} shows why structured incident records support later retrieval and analysis when the cause or consequence of a failure remains uncertain. \emph{FLARE-AI}~\cite{longpre2026flareai} identifies system identification, reproduction steps, and evidence as triage-relevant reporting dimensions, then uses structured, machine-readable reports to support coordination. Together, these studies suggest that retained red-team data should connect an attack trace to the evaluated target, the observed failure, and the conditions required to reproduce it.

\noindent\textbf{Safety Drift.} An original safety result does not transfer automatically to an adapted target. \emph{Fine-tuning Aligned Language Models Compromises Safety}~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels} establishes that even benign fine-tuning can degrade safety alignment. \emph{Keeping LLMs Aligned After Fine-Tuning}~\cite{lyu2024keepingaligned} further shows that the prompt templates used during fine-tuning and inference affect whether safety is retained, making the execution configuration part of a meaningful comparison. \emph{Safety Drift After Fine-Tuning}~\cite{khan2026safetydrift} extends this observation across 100 base and adapted models, finding large and heterogeneous changes in measured safety, with improvement on one evaluation sometimes accompanied by degradation on another. \emph{Preventing Safety Drift in Large Language Models via Coupled Weight and Activation Constraints}~\cite{peng2026cwac} then motivates update-time preservation by showing that benign adaptation can weaken previously learned refusal behavior. Continuous red teaming therefore needs to compare safety across target versions and repeatedly test the updated target; an earlier safety result should not be assumed valid after an update.

\noindent\textbf{Continuous Testing.} Retained replay and renewed discovery provide two complementary ways to track safety after updates. EvoJail~\cite{tang2026evojail} adapts attack generation to evolving model versions, while AutoRedTeamer~\cite{zhou2025autoredteamer} continually integrates newly discovered attack strategies. Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} constructs easily updatable safety benchmarks from generated jailbreak candidates, and AgenticEval~\cite{wang2026agenticeval} further explores self-evolving safety evaluation. Together, these approaches suggest that continuous red teaming should preserve historical failures for longitudinal comparison while periodically renewing the attacks or evaluation set. In practice, this also requires consistent execution, persistent records, and reusable evaluation workflows across changing targets and test conditions.

\noindent\textbf{Harness Capabilities.} A harness turns a red-team design into an executable experiment by connecting five operations. First, target adapters expose model APIs or application endpoints through a common runner. Second, attack orchestration selects prompts, seeds, budgets, and interaction steps. Third, judges convert responses, tool calls, or terminal outcomes into recorded decisions. Fourth, traces retain the prompts, responses, settings, model versions, and scores that produced those decisions. Finally, replay reruns a case under the original configuration or under a changed target, judge, or execution setting. In Table~\ref{tab:redteam_harnesses}, Generate and Adaptive describe how cases are constructed or updated; Judge and Multimodal describe what interactions can be assessed; Record and Replay describe whether the evidence needed for later comparison is preserved. A checkmark for Judge denotes an available judging interface or mechanism, not a claim that its labels are universally reliable.

\noindent\textbf{Representative Designs.} garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} combines probes, generators, and detectors through a plug-in structure for security testing. PyRIT~\cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit} organizes targets, attack orchestrators, converters, and scorers into a reusable workflow, including multimodal testing. promptfoo~\cite{promptfooDocs} makes providers, red-team strategies, and automated assertions configurable. Inspect AI~\cite{ukaisi2024inspect} couples model interfaces and scorers with tools and sandbox environments, then retains evaluation logs for rerunning and re-scoring. These design differences matter because a campaign may need only a prompt-level probe, or it may need to preserve a stateful trajectory through tools and an execution environment. In both cases, the harness supplies the configuration and evidence boundary that makes a reported result interpretable.

\noindent\textbf{Attack Engines and Evaluation Suites.} The harness does not decide which cases a campaign should run. Attack-generation systems such as GPTFuzzer~\cite{yu2023gptfuzzer}, APRT~\cite{jiang2024aprt}, AutoDAN~\cite{liu2024autodan}, and TAP~\cite{mehrotra2024tap} primarily contribute candidate attacks and adaptive search; in the role shown in Table~\ref{tab:redteam_harnesses}, they supply workload-generation capabilities rather than a general execution substrate. Evaluation suites and environments instead fix part of the comparison protocol. HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench} and JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench} provide common behaviors, attack artifacts, and evaluation procedures; AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} supplies an environment for agent security evaluation; and MM-SafetyBench~\cite{liu2024mmsafetybench} extends the workload to multimodal safety. This distinction explains why benchmarks belong in this section: they are inputs and protocols that a harness executes, records, and replays, not competing harnesses.

\noindent\textbf{Static and Renewable Workloads.} Fixed suites remain useful because stable cases and scoring procedures support direct comparison under controlled conditions. Their coverage, however, is bounded by predefined behaviors and artifacts while target systems and jailbreak strategies change. \emph{h4rm3l: A Dynamic Benchmark of Composable Jailbreak Attacks for LLM Safety Assessment}~\cite{doumbouya2025h4rm3l} represents attacks as composable transformations and synthesizes new attacks against the target, illustrating why static or templated sets can miss evolving combinations. Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} distills attacks into updateable benchmark artifacts. Fixed suites, renewable workloads, and harnesses are therefore complementary: the first stabilizes comparison conditions, the second refreshes test content, and the third preserves the configuration and traces needed to run either form over time. None of these resources determines which risks matter in a deployment or resolves a context-dependent judgment; those remaining tasks motivate the human role in the next subsection.



\subsection{Human-Machine Collaboration}

\noindent\textbf{Defining the Standard.} Automated red teaming becomes meaningful only after a campaign has a standard for what it is trying to establish. That standard specifies which risks enter the test space, which system boundary is under examination, what counts as harmful behavior or an unacceptable action, and what evidence is sufficient to support a finding. These choices determine both which failures can be discovered and how a reported result should be interpreted. STAR~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star} operationalizes human participation through parameterized instructions for red teamers, demographic matching for harm assessment, and arbitration of divergent perspectives. An interview study by Alvarado Garcia et al.~\cite{alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical} further shows that practitioners' choices about risk categories, scenarios, data creation, and evaluation shape which contexts and users are represented in red-team datasets.

\noindent\textbf{Revising the Standard.} Standards must also be grounded in the contexts in which a system will be used. Adversarial Nibbler~\cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler} uses crowdsourced human exploration to collect implicitly adversarial prompts and annotations of text-to-image harms that conventional testing can overlook. Going PLACES~\cite{rastogi2026goingplaces} works with local communities to contextualize local norms and construct culturally and linguistically grounded test cases. These contributions can revise a risk register rather than merely add prompts to a fixed benchmark: they may expose a missing harm category, an overlooked system boundary, or evidence that cannot be interpreted from a model response alone. DICES~\cite{NEURIPS2023_a74b697b} and D3CODE~\cite{mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code} document variation in safety and offensiveness judgments across annotators and cultural contexts. We therefore treat such disagreement as a signal that a criterion may need explicit review, rather than silently collapsing it into a single universal label.

\noindent\textbf{Automation as a Multiplier.} Automation is a multiplier of these human-defined standards, not an independent source of them. AART~\cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart} uses reusable and customizable recipes to scope and prioritize application-specific evaluation diversity, then generates larger adversarial evaluation sets. Bridging the Scale Gap~\cite{quaye2026bridging} starts from human-authored adversarial prompts and expands them using sociolinguistic strategies distilled from human red-team cases. Evaluation harnesses can then execute such workloads and preserve the resulting traces for comparison and replay~\cite{derczynski2024garak,lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect,promptfooDocs}. Automated judges may triage large batches, but their outputs remain conditional on the rubric, evidence, and threshold used to produce them; disagreement-heavy or high-impact cases require explicit audit and adjudication~\cite{souly2024strongreject,han2024wildguard,thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier}. Human authority is consequently continuous rather than exceptional: it sets the criteria, examines where those criteria fail or conflict, and revises the risk register, escalation rules, and disclosure decisions as evidence accumulates.

\subsection{Balancing Transparency and Exploitability}

Automated red teaming produces artifacts---jailbreak prompts, exploit chains, code, and test cases---and safety findings about a target. Responsible disclosure therefore raises two separable governance questions: which artifacts should be accessible to whom, and what information must be retained and communicated for a finding to remain auditable. Release Strategies and Social Impacts of Language Models~\cite{solaiman2019release} frames release as a staged decision rather than a binary choice. OpenAI's external-red-teaming account~\cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming} shows that access design and documentation are campaign-level decisions. In a cybersecurity setting, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} provide a concrete example: they withheld the full prompt and detailed reproduction steps from public release while offering access to researchers on request.

Reporting and coordination address a different question: whether a finding can be examined, acted on, and communicated responsibly. Coordinated Flaw Disclosure~\cite{cattell2024coordinatedflaw} treats verification and independent adjudication as parts of the disclosure process. Householder et al.~\cite{householder2024lessons} trace an AI/ML disclosure workflow from reporting and validation through coordination, remediation, and publication. Table~\ref{tab:redteam_disclosure} maps evidence-backed examples under these two parallel controls; it is not an ordinal or mutually exclusive release taxonomy.

\noindent\textbf{Access Control.} The artifact-handling examples in Table~\ref{tab:redteam_disclosure} do not form a fixed release ladder. They instead expose three decisions. \textbf{(1) Artifact sensitivity.} HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench}, JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}, and GPTFuzzer~\cite{yu2023gptfuzzer} make benchmark assets, repositories, or code publicly available for independent scrutiny. By contrast, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} withheld a full attack prompt and detailed reproduction procedure because those materials could lower the cost of harmful reuse. \textbf{(2) Recipient scope.} Ahmad et al.~\cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming} document campaigns in which participants receive different levels of system access; Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} illustrate research-on-request access. \textbf{(3) Public account.} The GPT-4 system card~\cite{openai2023gpt4systemcard} shows how a public high-level account can communicate risk and mitigation without publishing every reusable artifact. These examples motivate recording the sensitivity judgment, recipient scope, and public-versus-withheld status for each artifact. They do not establish universal thresholds for making those choices.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting Standard.} Finding accountability is a parallel requirement, not a consequence of a particular access level. A report should make it possible to assess what was tested, what evidence supports the finding, and what happened after it was raised. We therefore suggest recording \textbf{(1) the threat model and target configuration}, including model or system versions and attacker access; \textbf{(2) the evaluation procedure}, including the attack budget, judges, and success criteria; \textbf{(3) the evidence and artifact status}, stating what can be inspected publicly, obtained through a defined channel, or is withheld; and \textbf{(4) the disclosure state}, including validation, notification, remediation, and publication decisions. The bug-bounty study \emph{To Err Is AI}~\cite{mcgregor2025toerr} foregrounds review and the artifacts used to substantiate a report. Paeth et al.~\cite{paeth2025lessons} show why incident records need structure for later retrieval and analysis. FLARE-AI~\cite{longpre2026flareai} turns reporting requirements into a machine-readable schema for coordinated use. Together with the disclosure frameworks above, these works substantiate different parts of the proposed record. No common standard yet binds them to automated red-teaming artifacts; specifying and validating one remains an open problem.

\section{Open Problems and Future Directions}
\label{sec:agenda}

The central challenge for automated red teaming is no longer simply to produce a larger number of successful attacks. A useful red-teaming system must connect several tasks that are often evaluated separately: it must identify risks worth testing, discover failures under a specified system configuration, establish that an observed outcome is meaningful, and determine whether a subsequent intervention reduces that risk without creating unacceptable costs elsewhere. Earlier surveys have respectively organized this problem around an end-to-end red-teaming pipeline and around attack taxonomies and automated search strategies \cite{purpura-etal-2025-building,Lin_2025}. Building on these perspectives, this survey treats automated red teaming as a closed loop. The open problems below concern the links that make that loop credible: adaptive coverage, reliable judgment, system-level testing, and remediation that remains valid as targets evolve.

\subsection{Adaptive Risk Coverage Beyond Prompt Counts}

Automated methods can generate and test far more inputs than a manual red team, but the number of prompts, attempts, or successful jailbreaks is not itself a measure of risk coverage. A large collection may consist primarily of small reformulations that exploit the same weakness. Conversely, a small number of cases may reveal distinct failure mechanisms, such as instruction hijacking through retrieved content, unauthorized tool use, or persistent memory poisoning. Static benchmarks make controlled comparison possible, but their categories and examples necessarily bound the risks that they can reveal \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,tong2023multimon}. The field therefore needs coverage claims that distinguish variation in wording from variation in the underlying failure mechanism.

One research direction is to maintain a risk register throughout red-team search. Each confirmed case would be linked not only to a prompt and an outcome, but also to a provisional failure class, target boundary, interaction context, and evidence needed for replay. Clustering and novelty measures could then be evaluated by whether they separate cases with different causes or consequences, rather than merely producing linguistically diverse prompts. Work on diversity-oriented red teaming, quality-diversity search, and discovery-rate stopping rules offers useful starting points \cite{zhao2025diverct,li2024rtpe,jiang2024aprt,wu2025trident,zhou2025autoredteamer}. The unresolved question is how to decide that a newly generated case represents a new risk rather than a familiar attack expressed differently.

Coverage also depends on who defines the risk space. Application users, domain experts, and culturally situated participants can identify harms and contexts that do not appear in a fixed safety taxonomy. Participatory red teaming provides one route for incorporating such inputs, but automation must preserve the context that made a human-discovered case meaningful when it expands that case into a larger test set \cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star}. Future systems should therefore report both the risks that were searched and the sources from which those risks entered the search space. This would make coverage a qualified account of what has been explored, not a claim that all relevant risks have been exhausted.

\subsection{Decision-Grade Evaluation and Trustworthy Judges}

The value of a discovered attack depends on the quality of the judgment used to label it. Automated judges enable large-scale testing, yet different classifiers, rule sets, and LLM-based evaluators can assign different labels to the same response. Their disagreement with human raters is especially consequential for ambiguous, contextual, or culturally dependent cases \cite{souly2024strongreject,han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,zheng2023mtbench,chen2024humansjudge,thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier,eiras2025knowthyjudge}. In addition, outputs can be optimized to manipulate a judge rather than to demonstrate a meaningful target failure \cite{raina2024robustjudge,tong2025badjudge,shi2024optimization}. A reported attack success rate is therefore conditional on the evaluator, rubric, and decision threshold that produced it.

Future work should treat the evaluation protocol as part of the object being tested. For each safety claim, studies should specify what counts as harmful content, unsafe action, privacy leakage, policy circumvention, or task failure; identify which evidence a judge can observe; and record the judge version, prompt or rubric, and threshold. Human review need not replace automated evaluation for every case, but it can be used to calibrate judges on high-impact, ambiguous, and disagreement-heavy subsets. Such studies should report where automated and human decisions diverge, rather than reporting only a single aggregate agreement score.

This issue becomes more difficult for agents and multimodal systems. A text-only judge may label an intermediate response as safe even when the surrounding trajectory causes an unsafe action, while a final action signal may obscure whether the failure arose from the model, a tool, a permission policy, or the execution environment. Decision-grade evaluation must therefore connect the failure criterion to the system boundary under examination. The goal is not a universal safety judge. It is a protocol that makes clear which conclusion is supported by which evidence, and where that conclusion remains uncertain.

\subsection{Stateful and Compositional System Testing}

Many red-teaming evaluations still reduce success to a single prompt-response pair. This simplification becomes inadequate when a model retrieves untrusted material, retains memory, calls tools, or acts across a sequence of changing external states. In these settings, an early step may appear harmless but can shape a later decision, and the relevant failure may be an unauthorized action or irreversible effect rather than unsafe text. Benchmarks and environments for agents have begun to expose these properties, including indirect prompt injection, tool-mediated attacks, and multi-step task failures \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhan2024injecagent,ruan2024toolemu,chen2024agentpoison,zou2025poisonedrag,wang2025webinject}.

The next step is to evaluate trajectories as structured objects. A test should record the initial task, retrieved context, memory state, available permissions, tool calls, intermediate decisions, and final consequence. This makes it possible to ask whether an intervention prevents the original failure by blocking an unsafe action, by changing the model's interpretation of untrusted content, or simply by causing an earlier task failure. It also supports evaluations of containment and recovery, which are difficult to assess if the only reported result is a final text label.

Transferability belongs in this system-level problem rather than in a separate list of attack properties. An attack may transfer across base models but fail once a different retrieval pipeline, system prompt, permission boundary, or tool environment is introduced. Conversely, an apparently model-specific weakness may recur across applications that share a common control path. Future studies should use transfer matrices that vary these factors deliberately: attack representation, target model, alignment configuration, and deployment setting. Such designs would help distinguish failures attributable to a model from failures attributable to the application in which it is embedded \cite{liu2023houyi,wu2025darkfunction}. They would also make claims about model-family weaknesses more informative than an isolated source-target attack-success comparison.

\subsection{From Failure Discovery to Durable Safety Improvement}

Closing the loop requires more than replaying old jailbreaks after a model update. A remediation can suppress a known prompt while leaving its failure mechanism intact, reduce one class of harmful output while increasing over-refusal, or create a new weakness in a different application context. Iterative red teaming and safety tuning address part of this problem by generating new attacks against updated targets, while retained failures provide a stable reference set for longitudinal comparison \cite{zhou2025autoredteamer,zhang2025jbdistill}. The open question is how to establish that a measured improvement reflects a genuine reduction in risk rather than a change in the attacker, judge, or protocol.

A useful continuous-testing design should separate at least four forms of change: the target model or application, the attack generator and seed set, the evaluator, and the execution protocol. A change in any one of these can alter a reported safety score. Target updates can change safety behavior after downstream adaptation \cite{khan2026safetydrift,peng2026cwac}; refreshed attacks can reveal failures that an older suite could not find; and updated judges can relabel historical outputs. Versioned traces should therefore preserve enough information to replay a case under its original conditions and to rerun it under revised conditions. Comparing the two results can then reveal whether a failure disappeared, recurred, changed severity, or became unassessable because the protocol itself changed.

Remediation studies should also evaluate causal and practical consequences. A claim that a defense worked should identify the intervention boundary, use attack families that were not used to construct the defense, and report relevant costs such as benign-task utility, latency, human-review burden, or constrained tool functionality. Confirmed failures should become regression assets, but renewed discovery must continue alongside replay because replay alone tests only what is already known. This combination turns red teaming from a one-off demonstration of vulnerability into a process for maintaining evidence about residual risk.

\subsection{Comparable Evidence and Governed Disclosure}

The preceding directions require shared infrastructure as well as new attack algorithms. Evaluation harnesses, renewable benchmarks, and structured incident reports can preserve the configuration, traces, judgments, and replay conditions needed to compare results across targets and time \cite{mcgregor2025toerr,paeth2025lessons,longpre2026flareai}. Reporting should therefore identify the threat model, target configuration, attacker access, attack budget, evaluator and success criterion, and the status of released artifacts. Without these records, apparent differences between systems may reflect incompatible experimental conditions rather than differences in robustness.

At the same time, reproducibility cannot imply unrestricted release. Some artifacts, such as aggregate results, non-actionable schemas, and evaluation code, may support broad scrutiny. Others, including reliable jailbreak prompts, exploit chains, or detailed reproduction steps for autonomous agents, can lower the cost of misuse. Future work should develop disclosure procedures that connect artifact sensitivity to an access level and a minimum reporting package. The objective is to allow a safety claim to be examined even when the most directly reusable attack details are gated, summarized, or shared through coordinated disclosure.

Taken together, these directions shift the goal of automated red teaming from maximizing attack yield to producing durable safety evidence. A mature system would show what risks it searched, how it judged the outcomes, which model and application boundaries were implicated, what changed after remediation, and which parts of the evidence can be independently inspected. That standard is demanding, but it is the condition under which automated red teaming can support safety decisions rather than only demonstrate isolated failures.
\section{Conclusion}

Automated red teaming should be evaluated as a closed-loop safety-evaluation system rather than as a collection of isolated jailbreak techniques. A useful survey must therefore connect risk specification, generation, search, interaction, judgment, coverage, mitigation, and responsible operational practice across generative-model targets.
