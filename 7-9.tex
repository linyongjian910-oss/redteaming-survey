\begin{abstract}
The rapid deployment of generative models has created a need for automated red-teaming methods that discover failures beyond manually authored test cases. This survey focuses on automated red-teaming systems that generate, execute, and evaluate adversarial or safety-relevant interactions with generative models and model-based applications. We study automated red teaming across large language models, vision-language models, text-to-image and text-to-video systems, retrieval-augmented applications, and tool-using agents. We organize the literature around a closed-loop pipeline that connects risk objectives, test-case generation, interaction and execution, outcome judgment, failure triage, and mitigation. Within this pipeline, we unify red-teaming strategies by their search space, optimization signal, access assumptions, interaction horizon, and modality. We then compare evaluation metrics, benchmarks, automated judges, human-in-the-loop protocols, and reporting practices, with particular attention to attack success, harm severity, coverage, transferability, cost, and over-refusal. Finally, we identify open problems in long-horizon interaction, multimodal and multilingual testing, reliable evaluation, adaptive coverage, reproducibility, and the responsible use of automated red-teaming systems. The survey is intended to provide a common vocabulary and a practical roadmap for building scalable, evidence-based automated red-teaming evaluations.
\end{abstract}
\keywords{automated red teaming; generative models; large language models; vision-language models; text-to-image generation; AI safety; adversarial evaluation; jailbreaks}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation: Problem Scope}

\paragraph{Why red teaming matters.}
Generative models can fail under inputs that are rare, indirect, context-dependent, or outside the distribution represented by standard safety datasets. Red teaming treats safety assessment as proactive discovery: a tester constructs situations that may expose harmful compliance, privacy leakage, policy evasion, unsafe tool use, or other undesirable behavior before deployment.

\paragraph{Why automation matters.}
Manual red teaming provides valuable expertise but is expensive, difficult to reproduce, and limited in coverage. Automated red teaming extends this process by generating test cases, searching through interaction states, executing attacks, judging outputs, and retaining failures as regression tests. The central challenge is not merely to maximize attack success, but to discover diverse, relevant, and verifiable failure modes.

\subsection{Survey Positioning: Research Questions}

\paragraph{Scope.}
This survey focuses on automated or partially automated red-teaming systems for generative models. LLMs, VLMs/MLLMs, T2I/T2V systems, RAG applications, and tool-using agents are treated as target systems. General alignment, generic adversarial examples, and static safety benchmarks are included only when they support an automated red-teaming component.

\paragraph{Research questions.}
The survey is organized around five questions: how red-team objectives are specified; how test cases are generated and searched; how interactions and failures are evaluated; how methods transfer across models, modalities, and applications; and how discovered failures improve subsequent mitigation and regression testing.

\paragraph{Relation to prior surveys.}
Prior surveys provide complementary views of generative-model red teaming, LLM security, attack evaluation, practical red-teaming systems, and multimodal safety \cite{Lin_2025,Das_2025,Jabbar_2025,purpura-etal-2025-building,liu2024safetymultimodallargelanguage}. We use these works as field-level anchors, then reorganize the evidence around automation boundaries, closed-loop feedback, and target-system transfer.

\subsection{Contributions: Article Roadmap}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The survey makes four contributions:

\section{Unified Automated Red-Teaming Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Definitions: System Boundaries}

\paragraph{Automated red teaming.}
Define automated red teaming as goal-directed discovery of undesirable behavior in which one or more stages of test generation, selection, interaction, judgment, or iteration are automated under an explicit threat model.

\paragraph{Target system boundary.}
Separate the target model, safety layer, orchestration layer, retrieval layer, memory, tools, and deployed application. A discovered failure should be attributed to the smallest component or interaction responsible for it.

\subsection{Threat Model: Attack Conditions}

\paragraph{Attacker access.}
Distinguish black-box, gray-box, white-box, and application-level access. Record query limits, model knowledge, system-prompt visibility, gradient access, activation access, control over retrieved content, and control over tools or memory.

\paragraph{Risk objective.}
Classify objectives into harmful compliance, privacy leakage, prompt injection, policy evasion, misinformation, bias, copyright exposure, unsafe tool use, reliability failure, and over-refusal.

\paragraph{Interaction horizon.}
Separate single-turn, iterative, multi-turn, stateful, multi-agent, and long-horizon settings. The horizon determines whether the attack optimizes a response, a conversation trajectory, or an external action.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Pipeline: Evaluation Cycle}

\paragraph{Risk specification.}
Translate a policy, threat model, harm taxonomy, or application requirement into a test objective and an observable failure condition.

\paragraph{Test generation.}
Construct an initial population of prompts, scenarios, images, videos, retrieved documents, tool outputs, or multi-turn plans.

\paragraph{Search: Interaction.}
Select, mutate, optimize, or compose test cases while interacting with the target system. The search state may include prompts, conversation history, tool state, model feedback, and prior failures.

\paragraph{Outcome judgment.}
Determine whether the target behavior satisfies the failure condition using rules, classifiers, reward models, LLM judges, VLM judges, environments, or human annotators.

\paragraph{Triage: Regression.}
Cluster failures, estimate severity and novelty, remove duplicates, record reproducible attack traces, and convert confirmed failures into held-out regression tests.

\section{Automated Generation: Search Strategies}
\label{sec:generation}

\subsection{Scenario Construction: Initial Test Cases}

\paragraph{Seed prompts.}
Review expert prompts, policy-derived scenarios, safety datasets, attack libraries, synthetic harmful-intent descriptions, and application-specific user stories.

\paragraph{Scenario expansion.}
Cover semantic paraphrasing, translation, role-play, encoding, formatting, contextual embedding, instruction indirection, and risk-conditioned generation.

\paragraph{Diversity control.}
Compare novelty penalties, coverage constraints, population methods, quality-diversity search, clustering, and explicit demographic, cultural, or geographic balancing.

\subsection{Model-Based Generation: Attacker Models}

\paragraph{Attacker language models.}
Review zero-shot generation, proposer--judge loops, attacker--target interaction, model-written evaluations, and attacker fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Reverse generation.}
Discuss methods that begin from a harmful output, policy violation, or target behavior and search backward for an input that elicits it.

\paragraph{Multi-agent generation.}
Separate attacker, critic, judge, planner, and target roles. Analyze whether role separation improves exploration or introduces correlated errors.

\subsection{Algorithmic Search: Optimization Signals}

\paragraph{Fuzzing: Mutation.}
Cover seed selection, mutation operators, random search, evolutionary search, and black-box feedback.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning.}
Review attacker training with reward signals from toxicity classifiers, safety judges, target compliance, novelty, or environment outcomes.

\paragraph{Gradient-Level: Token-Level: Activation-Level Optimization.}
Describe white-box optimization of suffixes, tokens, continuous embeddings, image perturbations, or internal representations. Compare access requirements, transferability, realism, and cost.

\subsection{Adaptive Interaction: Stateful Search}

\paragraph{Iterative refinement.}
Analyze attacks that learn from refusals, partial compliance, judge feedback, or changes in target behavior.

\paragraph{Multi-turn search.}
Cover trust building, context shaping, refusal recovery, conversational state, memory persistence, and long-horizon planning.

\paragraph{Application-level search.}
Extend the search state to retrieved documents, web pages, tool outputs, user permissions, external state, and action consequences.

\section{Evaluation: Judgment: Benchmarking}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Failure Definition: Success Conditions}

\paragraph{Behavioral success.}
Distinguish policy violation, harmful content, unsafe action, privacy leakage, misinformation, reliability failure, and evaluator disagreement. Every benchmark should define the target behavior before reporting attack success.

\paragraph{Severity: Usefulness.}
Measure not only whether a guardrail was bypassed, but also whether the response contains actionable, relevant, and sufficiently complete harmful information or performs an unsafe action.

\subsection{Automated Judges: Human Agreement}

\paragraph{Judge families.}
Compare keyword rules, lexical matching, safety classifiers, reward models, LLM-as-judge, VLM-as-judge, structured rubrics, environment success signals, and human review.

\paragraph{Judge reliability.}
Report calibration, inter-rater agreement, judge disagreement, false positives, false negatives, judge hacking, and agreement with human judgments.

\subsection{Metrics: Experimental Comparability}

\paragraph{Attack effectiveness.}
Cover attack success rate, harmfulness score, refusal rate, compliance quality, and task-level success.

\paragraph{Search quality.}
Cover coverage, diversity, novelty, marginal discovery rate, transferability, robustness, query efficiency, latency, and compute cost.

\paragraph{Safety--utility balance.}
Report over-refusal, benign-task utility, calibration, safe completion quality, and the trade-off between harmfulness reduction and helpfulness.

\subsection{Benchmarks: Protocol Design}

\paragraph{Static datasets.}
Compare behavior sets, harmful instructions, refusal suites, adversarial examples, multimodal pairs, and application-specific test collections.

\paragraph{Dynamic environments.}
Discuss extensible harnesses, agent environments, tool-use simulators, external-state tasks, adaptive attacks, and continual benchmark updates.

\paragraph{Reproducibility checklist.}
Require model version, system prompt, access condition, sampling parameters, query budget, random seeds, judge version, annotation protocol, compute cost, attack artifacts, and release policy.

\section{Failure Remediation: Safety Improvement}
\label{sec:mitigation}

\subsection{Learning from Red-Team Failures}

\paragraph{Remediation as a Closed Loop.}
Failure discovery need not be the terminal stage of automated red teaming. Red-team traces can expose concrete conditions under which a target model produces harmful or otherwise undesirable behavior, creating evidence for subsequent safety tuning. Ganguli et al. frame red teaming as a process that discovers, measures, and attempts to reduce harmful outputs, and release 38,961 red-team attacks for further analysis \cite{ganguli2022redteaming}. Perez et al. demonstrate that one language model can generate test cases for another and uncover tens of thousands of offensive replies across multiple failure types \cite{perez2022redteaming}. Their work primarily establishes scalable failure discovery and characterization, rather than direct remediation of the target model. MART connects these stages by generating adversarial prompts, using them in target-model safety fine-tuning, and launching new attacks against the updated target \cite{ge2024mart}. This progression turns red-team evidence into a potential input to model improvement, motivating the conversion of confirmed failures into training supervision.

\paragraph{From Failure Traces to Training Supervision.}
Recent systems reuse automatically generated adversarial prompts as safety-training data rather than treating them only as test cases. MART directly fine-tunes the target model using safety-aligned data constructed around adversarially elicited prompts before launching another attack round \cite{ge2024mart}. Related work reuses diverse generated attacks for safety tuning and evaluates the resulting models against other reinforcement-learning-based red-teamers \cite{lee2025diverseattacks}, while DiveR-CT links the diversity and composition of collected attacks to downstream blue-team tuning \cite{zhao2025diverct}. Taken together, these studies suggest that, from a closed-loop red-teaming perspective, the common element is not a particular optimization procedure but the reuse of failure-inducing inputs to construct subsequent safety supervision. Once these traces are reused for model updates, the red-team generator begins to shape the model's evolving safety-training distribution.

\paragraph{Targeted Repair and the Safety--Utility Trade-off.}
Targeted safety supervision can substantially improve measured safety, but remediation should be evaluated jointly with its effects on benign behavior. Safety-Tuned LLaMAs reports that adding roughly 3\% safety examples, corresponding to a few hundred demonstrations, substantially improves safety without significant degradation on standard capability and helpfulness benchmarks \cite{bianchi2024safetytuned}. The same study finds exaggerated safety under more extensive safety tuning, including refusals on prompts that are safe but superficially resemble unsafe requests. RASS addresses this boundary problem by automatically generating and selecting prompts near the safety decision boundary for targeted over-refusal mitigation \cite{pan2025overrefusal}. XSTest provides a complementary evaluation protocol with 250 safe prompts and 200 unsafe contrasts for measuring exaggerated safety \cite{rottger2024xstest}. These findings show that safety improvement cannot be measured by harmful-query refusal alone. The resulting safety--utility trade-off motivates a shift from one-shot safety tuning toward training procedures in which new failures are repeatedly generated against the updated model.

\paragraph{From One-Shot Repair to Adaptive Adversarial Training.}
A fixed set of failure traces may support an initial repair, but it cannot by itself reveal how vulnerabilities change after the target model is updated. Automated red teaming becomes part of the training-data generation process when new attacks are generated against the revised model and fed back into subsequent updates. MART provides the clearest example: an adversarial model generates prompts that elicit unsafe responses, the target model is safety-fine-tuned using safety-aligned data constructed around the resulting adversarial prompts, and the attacker then searches against the updated target in the next round \cite{ge2024mart}. MART reports up to an 84.7\% reduction in violation rate after four rounds, while helpfulness on non-adversarial prompts remains stable. HarmBench provides a complementary adversarial-training setting through R2D2, which maintains persistent test cases that are dynamically optimized during training and evaluates attack-defense performance across a standardized collection of methods and models \cite{mazeika2024harmbench}. Learning Diverse Attacks and DiveR-CT provide supporting evidence that the diversity and composition of generated attacks affect downstream safety tuning, although they do not implement the same attacker--defender loop as MART \cite{lee2025diverseattacks,zhao2025diverct}. Once generated attacks are reused for model updates, red-team search helps define the model's evolving safety-training distribution.

\paragraph{Robustness to Training Attacks Is Not General Robustness.}
If automated attacks shape the safety-training distribution, evaluation must also ask what happens outside that distribution. Jailbroken identifies competing objectives and mismatched generalization as two failure modes through which safety training can remain vulnerable despite extensive safety training \cite{wei2023jailbroken}. Direct counterevidence comes from Simple Adaptive Attacks, which reports 100\% attack success on several tested safety-aligned models, including R2D2, when the attack is adapted to the target model \cite{andriushchenko2025adaptive}. A different distribution shift appears in the past-tense reformulation study: on GPT-4o, the reported success rate increases from 1\% for direct requests to 88\% after 20 past-tense reformulation attempts \cite{andriushchenko2025pasttense}. Explicitly adding past-tense examples can improve defense against that specific reformulation, but this result reinforces rather than resolves the generalization problem. Safety Reasoning with Guidelines similarly reports that direct refusal training can rely on superficial mappings under out-of-distribution attacks, while guideline-based reasoning improves the reported OOD performance \cite{wang2025safetyreasoning}. Together, these findings indicate that remediation may fail to generalize beyond the failure patterns represented during training.

\paragraph{Evaluating Remediation Beyond the Training Distribution.}
Training-time remediation should therefore be evaluated along three dimensions: held-out harmful behaviors, unseen or adaptive attack strategies, and benign utility. HarmBench standardizes comparisons across 18 red-teaming methods and 33 target models and defenses, while also showing that no tested defense is uniformly effective \cite{mazeika2024harmbench}. JailbreakBench complements this setting with an open collection of attack artifacts, 100 behaviors, and standardized threat-model and scoring protocols \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}. These benchmarks support reproducible comparison, but neither should be treated as a complete measure of deployment risk. Benign behavior requires separate evaluation. XSTest pairs 250 safe prompts with 200 unsafe contrasts to measure exaggerated safety \cite{rottger2024xstest}, while Safety-Tuned LLaMAs reports that stronger safety tuning can produce refusals on superficially similar safe prompts \cite{bianchi2024safetytuned}. Past-tense reformulations and adaptive attacks provide additional tests beyond the remediation-time distribution \cite{andriushchenko2025pasttense,andriushchenko2025adaptive}. No single benchmark covers all three dimensions. Training-time remediation should be viewed as expanding the region over which safety behavior generalizes, rather than merely eliminating the failures used as training examples. Retaining confirmed failures as reusable regression cases is discussed in Section~7.4.

\subsection{Turning Safety Judgments into Runtime Control}

\paragraph{Runtime intervention.}
Training-time mitigation changes model behavior through additional optimization, whereas inference-time mitigation turns safety judgments into runtime controls while leaving the target parameters fixed or largely unchanged. In the closed-loop view of automated red teaming, evaluator outputs that were previously used to identify failures can instead become runtime signals for filtering or controlling generation. This shift connects the judge, feedback, and generation components of the red-team loop to deployment-time guardrails.

\paragraph{Evaluator-to-guard transfer.}
A first reuse pattern moves the red-team judge into the deployment path. WildGuard unifies prompt harmfulness detection, response safety-risk detection, and refusal detection for language models, while Llama Guard and ShieldGemma provide related input and output moderation capabilities \cite{han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,zeng2024shieldgemma}. Such evaluator-like models can be placed before the target to inspect user inputs, after the target to inspect generated responses, or at both locations. NeMo Guardrails makes this connection explicit by linking moderation decisions to programmable runtime rails \cite{rebedea2023nemo}. Once placed in the deployment path, the evaluator's prediction becomes an intervention signal rather than merely an offline assessment. This transition is not automatic, however. The calibration study of LLM-based guard models reports overconfidence, jailbreak-induced miscalibration, and variation across response models \cite{liu2025guardcalibration}. Reliable deployment therefore requires evaluating calibration and operating thresholds in addition to aggregate classification accuracy.

\paragraph{Locations of runtime control.}
Safety judgments can enter the runtime pipeline at different locations. Before generation, a system can analyze intent, rewrite a prompt, add policy guidance, or route the request through a restricted flow. Intention Analysis uses a two-stage inference-only procedure in which the model first analyzes the essential intent of an input and then conditions the final response on that analysis \cite{zhang2025intention}. NeMo Guardrails provides a broader framework for connecting such checks to runtime controls \cite{rebedea2023nemo}. During generation, the safety signal can modify token probabilities or internal representations. SafeDecoding adjusts decoding probabilities using safety-aware token-level information, whereas Jailbreak Antidote steers a sparse subset of hidden states along a safety direction during inference \cite{xu2024safedecoding,shen2025jailbreakantidote}. After generation, output-side guards can classify generated responses, while runtime frameworks can use these judgments to block, route, or otherwise control unsafe outputs \cite{han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,rebedea2023nemo}. These mechanisms differ in model access, latency, controllability, and dependence on the target architecture, but they share a shift from passive evaluation toward active runtime control.

\paragraph{Feedback-conditioned defensive generation.}
A similar feedback structure appears in automated attack refinement. Automated attackers use target or judge feedback to update subsequent attacks, whereas inference-time defenses can use safety feedback to condition ongoing or subsequent generation. SafeDecoding and Immune illustrate this pattern through token-level safety signals and safety rewards, respectively \cite{xu2024safedecoding,ghosal2025immune}. Intention Analysis uses an intermediate interpretation of attacker intent to control a subsequent response pass, while Reasoning-to-Defend illustrates a related strategy in which safety reflections and safety-pivot tokens influence response generation \cite{zhang2025intention,zhu2025reasoningtodefend}. The analogy is structural rather than algorithmic: the two processes use feedback-conditioned generation toward opposite objectives, and several defensive methods rely on target-specific decoding or trained behaviors rather than a model-agnostic judge-and-regenerate wrapper.

\paragraph{Cross-modal safety signals.}
Multimodal systems make the location of the safety signal less obvious. A text-only guard may miss a malicious image paired with an innocuous-looking instruction, a harmful meaning created by image-text interaction, or an attack encoded in a modality-specific representation. One line of work addresses image-side adversarial perturbations before they reach the target model. CIDER, for example, uses cross-modal similarity to detect maliciously perturbed image inputs and is designed to operate independently of the target MLLM \cite{xu2024cider}. Multimodal moderation extends the judge itself beyond text: Llama Guard 3 Vision classifies image-understanding conversations, while MULTIGUARD introduces OMNIGUARD for safety moderation across language, image, and audio inputs \cite{chi2024llamaguardvision,verma2025multiguard}. Immune represents a different intervention point by using a multimodal safety reward to guide decoding \cite{ghosal2025immune}. These methods suggest three complementary guard locations: cross-modal input detection, multimodal moderation, and decoding-time safety guidance. They do not imply that prompt-side detection provides complete coverage of generated images, videos, or audio.

\paragraph{Generative media and audio.}
For generative media, input-side filtering is insufficient when risk emerges during generation or only becomes observable in the generated output. SAFREE represents generation-time intervention for text-to-image and text-to-video systems by detecting toxic concepts in the text-embedding space, steering generation away from unsafe subspaces, and applying self-validation during diffusion sampling \cite{yoon2025safree}. T2VShield extends protection across prompt processing and generated video by combining prompt rewriting with local and global analysis of temporal and cross-modal inconsistencies \cite{liang2025t2vshield}. The need for output- and temporal-aware protection is reinforced from the attack side by TEAR. TEAR constructs textually innocuous prompts that exploit temporal event sequencing to elicit policy-violating video content and reports high attack success across open-source and commercial T2V systems \cite{he2026tear}. TEAR is not an inference-time defense, but it shows why prompt-level or static-content filters can miss harms that emerge through temporal composition.

Audio exposes a related but different gap because safety-relevant information can reside in both semantic content and waveform-level signals. SALMONN-Guard jointly inspects speech, audio, and text to intercept compositional attacks before the target model is queried \cite{yang2026salmonnguard}. AudioGuard further separates waveform-level and semantic content risks and combines specialized decisions under different threat models \cite{kang2026audioguard}. Among the audio defenses reviewed here, existing methods concentrate on input-side interception, whereas generated-audio moderation remains comparatively underrepresented. Across text, image, video, and audio systems, the appropriate guard location therefore follows the location of the risk signal: textual intent, visual content, cross-modal interaction, generated output, acoustic structure, or temporal evolution.

\paragraph{Guardrails as an attack surface.}
Guardrails consequently become part of the attack surface. Calibration failures can undermine threshold-based decisions \cite{liu2025guardcalibration}, while multimodal and generative-media studies show that harmful behavior can arise from modality interactions or become observable only in generated outputs \cite{chi2024llamaguardvision,liang2025t2vshield,he2026tear}. Guardrail evaluation should therefore extend beyond accuracy on a fixed attack set to include calibration, benign utility, runtime cost, and robustness to adaptive or transferred attacks. Inference-time guardrails do not eliminate the need for automated red teaming; they expand its target from the base model to the full runtime safety pipeline. Confirmed guardrail bypasses can then become regression cases for continuous red teaming in Section~7.4.

\subsection{Securing the Application Beyond the Model}

\paragraph{Model and system safety.}
Model-level safety does not by itself establish system-level safety. The safeguards discussed in Sections 7.1 and 7.2 primarily reduce the probability that a model produces an unsafe response or follows a harmful instruction. Once the model is connected to retrieval, tools, persistent state, and external services, however, a failure can also alter control flow, exercise an unauthorized capability, disclose data, or change an external state. AgentDojo makes this distinction operational by evaluating agents against security properties and tool consequences rather than only inspecting their final text \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}. A central application-level requirement is therefore to separate information that the model may read from authority that can redefine the task. In indirect prompt-injection settings, the system-level failure is not merely exposure to untrusted content, but allowing that content to acquire control authority over application behavior. CaMeL implements this separation by extracting control and data flows from the trusted query and restricting the influence of untrusted retrieved content on program flow; it further uses capabilities to constrain unauthorized information flows \cite{debenedetti2025camel}. These mechanisms preserve a distinction between data that informs execution and instructions that control it. External data may inform the task without being allowed to redefine it.

\paragraph{Independent system controls.}
Even if an injection crosses this trust boundary and manipulates the model's behavior, an independent boundary around execution can still limit the resulting effect. The first mechanism is isolation from real effects. IsolateGPT separates application execution and mediates interactions between applications and the surrounding system through controlled interfaces and permissions, providing a concrete example of effect isolation \cite{wu2025isolategpt}. The second mechanism is authorization before execution. The Task Shield checks whether instructions and proposed tool calls contribute to the user's specified goals, while ToolSafe detects unsafe tool invocations before execution and feeds safety judgments back into the agent's reasoning \cite{jia2025taskshield,mou2026toolsafe}. Together, these approaches insert independent system controls between model decisions and external effects, through isolation, mediation, or pre-execution authorization. This separation matters because a model decision and a system authorization are different events. A model may decide that it should send an email, read a file, or invoke a service, while an independent application layer can still check whether the operation serves the user's task and falls within the authority granted to the agent. The model proposes an action; the system determines whether that action is executable. Evidence from coding-agent authorization further cautions against delegating this decision to the model itself: AuthBench reports both omitted permissions needed for a task and grants of unused or sensitive access, and additional inference-time reasoning does not remove the mismatch \cite{do2026leastprivilege}. These results support enforcing authorization through independent system controls rather than relying on the model alone to determine its own privileges.

\paragraph{Consequences and recovery.}
At the application boundary, failure should therefore be measured by its consequence, not only by the model's decision. Application-level evaluation should track unauthorized actions, data exfiltration, and protected-state changes rather than textual behavior alone. AgentDojo evaluates these questions in realistic tool-using workflows, while ToolEmu uses an LM-emulated sandbox to test high-stakes tool-use failures before real effects occur \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,ruan2024toolemu}. The literature reviewed here is substantially more developed around preventing or isolating unsafe actions than around recovery after external side effects. Once an agent has sent an email, modified a permission, or written to an external service, the effect may no longer be fully under the application's control. Recovery is consequently harder than blocking a proposed action before execution, especially when the side effect is irreversible or crosses service boundaries. Early transactional designs such as Cordon explore staged effects and commit/rollback boundaries, but evidence for general recovery remains limited \cite{chen2026cordon}.

\paragraph{System-level red teaming.}
A system-level red-team test should ask whether untrusted data acquired control authority, whether a compromised model obtained unauthorized capabilities, and whether prohibited effects were executed or contained. Application-level mitigation therefore extends automated red teaming from testing model behavior to testing whether application-level security properties survive model failure. Adaptive attacks against system-control mechanisms remain under-tested, while post-execution recovery from durable side effects remains comparatively immature.

\subsection{Continuous Testing After Remediation}

\paragraph{Failure promotion.}
Continuous red teaming turns a one-time remediation result into a replayable safety workflow. A raw attack success is not sufficient for reliable regression testing. We argue that a replayable failure case should preserve the observed behavior, target configuration, evaluator, threat condition, and reproduction context. Additional metadata such as severity and deduplication status can support triage and prioritization. HarmBench provides a standardized evaluation framework with reusable test cases, target models, defenses, and attack methods, while JailbreakBench provides an open collection of behaviors, attack artifacts, wrappers, and scoring protocols \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. These resources motivate reusable evaluation artifacts, but neither should be treated as a complete software-style regression system. In particular, retaining a successful prompt without recording the observed behavior, evaluator, threat condition, and reproduction context preserves an input rather than a replayable failure case.

\paragraph{Version-aware replay.}
Regression replay should therefore be version-aware. A previously observed failure may disappear, recur, or change in severity as the model or its mitigation layers are updated. The GPT-4 System Card describes iterative red teaming across model versions and repeated testing as new mitigation and control layers are incorporated \cite{openai2023gpt4systemcard}. More generally, for model-based applications, the replay target is the evaluated system configuration rather than the model checkpoint alone. Changes in surrounding components such as policies, retrieval, tools, or evaluators can alter the observed outcome; this broader system-level view follows the component boundaries introduced earlier in the survey. A practical case should therefore preserve configuration identifiers and evaluation conditions, so that recurrence, disappearance, and changes in severity can be distinguished from changes caused by an altered judge or execution environment.

\paragraph{Retained versus renewed testing.}
Fixed regression cases and renewed adversarial discovery answer different questions. Retained cases ask whether previously confirmed failures recur after a change; they cannot by themselves show that new attack strategies have not opened a different failure region. AutoRedTeamer addresses the renewal side through memory-guided attack selection and a strategy proposer that continuously discovers and integrates new attack vectors \cite{zhou2025autoredteamer}. Jailbreak Distillation renews the evaluation artifact instead: it distills attacks into updateable safety benchmarks and supports rerunning the construction pipeline as new models and attack methods become available \cite{zhang2025jbdistill}. The distinction is consequential: AutoRedTeamer renews the attacker, whereas Jailbreak Distillation renews the benchmark. Passing a retained regression suite provides evidence that the retained failures do not recur under the tested configuration, not evidence of broad safety coverage. Neither paper alone establishes a complete regression workflow for arbitrary model-system changes.

\paragraph{Continuous red-teaming loop.}
A mature remediation loop therefore combines two complementary mechanisms: persistence, which retains verified failures to detect recurrence, and renewal, which updates attacks or evaluation artifacts to probe beyond known failures \cite{openai2023gpt4systemcard,zhou2025autoredteamer,zhang2025jbdistill}. In this view, mitigation is not the endpoint of red teaming but a state transition in a continuing evaluation loop: retained failure cases are replayed against the mitigated or updated configuration, while renewed search tests whether the repair generalizes beyond the cases that motivated it. Questions about how taxonomies, judges, and evaluation targets can evolve without undermining longitudinal comparability are taken up in the future directions below.

\section{Practice: Tools: Responsible Use}
\label{sec:practice}

\subsection{Operationalizing Red Teaming with Evaluation Harnesses}

\paragraph{Framework capabilities.}
Evaluation harnesses operationalize automated red-teaming loops by connecting target or model adapters, attack and probe modules, evaluators, execution controls, and result logging. Among the harness-oriented systems examined here, this integration layer is not tied to one attack method. garak, PyRIT, Inspect AI, and promptfoo illustrate the pattern: each documents mechanisms for binding target systems, applying probes or evaluation logic, managing runs or concurrency, and storing outputs or scores \cite{derczynski2024garak,lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect,promptfooDocs}. The common abstraction is therefore an executable red-team workflow that can organize targets, evaluation, execution, and records across runs rather than a single jailbreak algorithm.

\paragraph{Interaction scope.}
Harnesses differ most in the scope of interaction they can execute. Evaluation scope ranges from prompt-response probing to modular attacks, multimodal targets, tools, sandboxes, and agent environments. PyRIT extends red-team workflows through modular attacks and multimodal targets \cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit}, while Inspect AI exposes tasks, solvers, tools, scorers, sandboxes, and execution logs \cite{ukaisi2024inspect}. AgentDojo and CybersecurityBenchmarks further illustrate evaluation in environments where success depends on tool use or application-level behavior \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,metaCybersecurityBenchmarks}. The relevant distinction is not simply the number of supported features, but how much of the target system can be brought inside the executable evaluation loop.

\paragraph{Benchmark versus harness.}
Benchmark and harness are functional roles rather than stages in a replacement sequence. Fixed benchmark artifacts stabilize test cases, behaviors, and scoring protocols for standardized comparison. MM-SafetyBench provides this role for multimodal safety evaluation \cite{liu2024mmsafetybench}. Extensible harnesses instead provide replaceable targets, attacks, evaluators, and execution components: PyRIT supports modular targets, attacks, scorers, memory, and multimodal evaluation \cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit}, while Inspect AI supports replaceable model APIs, tasks, solvers, tools, scorers, logs, sandboxing, and re-scoring \cite{ukaisi2024inspect}. Hybrid systems complicate a strict benchmark--harness dichotomy. HarmBench combines standardized behaviors and evaluation artifacts with extensible red-team methods and model configurations \cite{mazeika2024harmbench}, while JailbreakBench combines fixed artifacts, a threat model, scoring functions, model wrappers, and artifact-level attack submissions \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}. The distinction is therefore functional rather than categorical: benchmarks stabilize what is tested, whereas harnesses organize how tests are executed, and modern systems can serve both roles.

\paragraph{Persistence across runs.}
Logging, reproducibility, and regression form increasingly strong levels of evaluation persistence. Logging records what happened in a run, including prompts, outputs, scores, reports, and metadata. Reproducibility preserves enough configuration, model and dataset identifiers, seeds, or artifacts to rerun or compare an evaluation. Here, we use regression testing to refer to deliberately re-executing retained tests or confirmed failures after changes to a model, system, configuration, or defense. The examined systems provide combinations of these mechanisms through saved artifacts, evaluation sets, repeat options, released prompts or completions, and standardized materials \cite{derczynski2024garak,openaiEvals,promptfooDocs,mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,debenedetti2024agentdojo,metaCybersecurityBenchmarks,liang2023helm}. Inspect AI provides a particularly explicit example through persistent evaluation logs, saved run configuration, and re-scoring support, which enable repeated analysis of prior evaluations \cite{ukaisi2024inspect}. Explicit regression workflows are less uniformly exposed than logging or rerun support in the systems reviewed here; this observation describes a documentation and workflow gap, not evidence that undocumented systems lack regression capability.

\paragraph{The automation boundary.}
Evaluation harnesses therefore do more than simplify implementation: they shape which parts of the red-team loop can be executed, recorded, compared, and repeated at scale. As evaluations move toward multimodal and agentic systems, this infrastructure increasingly defines the practical scope of automated red teaming. Expanding executable coverage does not remove the need for human expertise in defining risks, validating judges, and interpreting novel failures, which motivates the participatory-testing discussion in Section~8.2.

\subsection{Where Human Expertise Still Matters}

\paragraph{Expanding the risk space.}
Human participation can expand the red-team risk space by changing what is searched for, rather than merely adding tests within a fixed objective \cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler,rastogi2026goingplaces,weidinger-etal-2024-star}. Adversarial Nibbler used crowdsourced red teaming to elicit implicitly adversarial text-to-image prompts, revealing non-obvious, long-tail safety issues and novel attack strategies within that setting \cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler}. Local knowledge can likewise change the content and interpretation of tests: Going PLACES elicited culturally and linguistically situated prompts and annotations in Ghana, Nigeria, Karnataka, and Punjab, while CulturalBench combined human-written and human-verified questions across 45 global regions through a process inspired by Human--AI red teaming \cite{rastogi2026goingplaces,chiu-etal-2025-culturalbench}. Human input can also shape the search objective itself. STAR operationalizes human-authored, parameterized risk rules, while a participatory study in South Korea shows how lived experience informed stereotype-targeted attack strategies \cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,kim2026darkbright}. These studies do not imply exhaustive coverage or general superiority over automated discovery. Instead, they show that human participation can introduce risks and contexts omitted by predefined risk objectives \cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler,rastogi2026goingplaces,chiu-etal-2025-culturalbench}. If human participation helps determine what should be tested, automation determines how broadly those specifications can be exercised.

\paragraph{Scaling human-specified risks.}
The literature repeatedly adopts a complementary division of labor in which humans define risks, seeds, contexts, or priorities, while automated systems generate, execute, and screen tests at scale \cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart,perez2022redteaming,quaye2026bridging,alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}. At the specification stage, AART encodes application context, policies, harms, populations, regions, and priorities as human-defined recipes that a language model expands into adversarial prompts. Bridging the Scale Gap similarly expands human-authored seeds with human-derived strategies, increasing geographic and demographic coverage without proportional human authoring effort \cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart,quaye2026bridging}. At the scaling stage, Perez et al. use attacker models, classifiers, reinforcement learning, and clustering, but find that optimizing attack effectiveness can reduce diversity; greater automation therefore does not automatically broaden coverage \cite{perez2022redteaming}. In operational practice, interviewees describe selecting and ranking harms, inspecting outputs, and handling ambiguous cases while using synthetic generation and automated judges to support scale \cite{alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}. Across these systems, automation primarily scales human-defined risks and seeds rather than replacing human specification. The literature supports complementarity more clearly than any fixed division of labor. Scaling test generation also shifts the bottleneck toward judging the resulting failures.

\paragraph{Human validation of automated judgments.}
Automated judges enable large-scale failure screening, while human expertise can enter the judging workflow at targeted points \cite{perez2022redteaming,alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}. First, human labels can validate an automated judge on a bounded sample before large-scale use, as in Perez et al.'s evaluation of an offensive-language classifier against human annotations \cite{perez2022redteaming}. Second, uncertain, novel, or context-sensitive cases can be escalated for human interpretation. Build it Break it Fix it shows that offensive-language detection in dialogue depends critically on conversational context; Going PLACES uses local annotations for culturally situated harms; and Adversarial Nibbler combines machine safety annotations with richer human annotations of harm types and attack styles \cite{dinan-etal-2019-build,rastogi2026goingplaces,quaye2024adversarialnibbler}. Third, disagreement can be adjudicated without treating human judgment as an unquestionable gold standard. STAR gives an arbitrator access to annotator reasoning, DICES preserves individual and demographic ratings alongside expert labels, and D3CODE analyzes culturally structured disagreement rather than collapsing it during collection \cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,NEURIPS2023_a74b697b,mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code}. These mechanisms support selective human review where context or disagreement matters, while leaving the appropriate escalation criteria largely unresolved \cite{perez2022redteaming,weidinger-etal-2024-star,alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}.

\paragraph{Limits of human scaling.}
Human red teaming is costly and difficult to scale across large or continuously changing test spaces. Practitioner studies report that recruiting suitable evaluators and maintaining manual review throughout the testing pipeline require substantial human effort, making exhaustive human evaluation impractical at scale \cite{alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}. Consistent with this constraint, hybrid workflows use automation for high-volume generation and screening while reserving human effort for risk definition and difficult judgments \cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart,perez2022redteaming,alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical}. Human effort is therefore a limited resource within the red-teaming pipeline, making selective involvement more practical than exhaustive manual review.

\subsection{Balancing Transparency and Exploitability}

Automated red teaming produces artifacts ranging from evaluation logs and benchmark cases to optimized prompts, attacker models, and reproduction triggers. These materials can support scientific scrutiny, but they can also lower the cost of misuse. External red teaming can itself create information hazards when it exposes novel jailbreaks or harmful-content techniques \cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming}. Recent offensive-security evaluations illustrate why this concern can become operationally relevant \cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack}.

A useful way to characterize artifact sensitivity is by how directly an artifact enables exploitation, including its actionability, reusability, and the expertise or search effort required to use it. These dimensions are best viewed as an organizing lens rather than a formal risk score. The reviewed studies also suggest why file type alone is insufficient. Qi et al. gated realistic harmful benchmark questions while reporting results on the public AdvBench benchmark, while offensive-security studies restricted prompts, source documents, or agent implementations and still reported the resulting evaluations \cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}. Together, these practices suggest that sensitivity depends more on operational reusability than on artifact format alone.

The reviewed studies use markedly different release practices. Harmful evaluation sets have been placed behind manually reviewed access or withheld, while offensive-security studies have restricted prompts, implementation details, or target identities and disclosed sensitive findings directly to providers \cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}. These practices vary along four main dimensions: content, access, timing, and disclosure process. Solaiman et al.'s staged release of GPT-2 provides an earlier precedent for conditioning availability on evolving risk assessment, but not a ready-made standard for red-team artifacts \cite{solaiman2019release}.

These restrictions need not eliminate scientific transparency. A study can remain auditable by documenting the threat model, experimental setup, evaluation procedure, and representative non-sensitive evidence even when prompts, source documents, target identities, or agent implementations are not public \cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyexploit,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}. This does not guarantee full reproducibility, but it preserves information needed to scrutinize the reported claim. Open-release benchmarks such as Cybench, HarmBench, and the red-team dataset of Ganguli et al. show that public artifacts can support inspection and standardized comparison when tasks, threat models, and evaluation protocols are explicitly scoped. These practices do not establish that the same release choice is appropriate for directly reusable exploit chains \cite{zhang2025cybenchframeworkevaluatingcybersecurity,mazeika2024harmbench,ganguli2022redteaming}.

Based on these practices, we argue that reporting should preserve the evaluation trace from the threat model and attack-generation process through execution and evaluation to the observed failure. Relevant model or environment versions, evaluation criteria, and cost should be reported where they affect interpretation. If remediation is part of the study, the trace should also include the mitigation and a held-out regression result; an attack-discovery study need not make a remediation claim that it did not evaluate. The reviewed studies make different portions of the setup and evidence available \cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming,qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}. When artifacts are restricted, authors should state clearly which experimental details and materials remain available for independent scrutiny. Shared rules for access rights, release timing, benchmark maintenance, and public accountability remain a field-level governance problem taken up in the future directions below.

\section{Open Problems and Future Directions}
\label{sec:agenda}

\noindent\textbf{\(\blacktriangleright\) Coverage beyond fixed benchmarks.}
Benchmarks such as HarmBench \cite{mazeika2024harmbench} measure predefined behavior sets. Open-ended testing still needs a rule for distinct failures and an estimate of what has not been found. Chao-style estimators \cite{chao1984unseenclasses} address the latter question, but safety testing also needs severity-aware discovery curves that separate a stalled search from broad coverage.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{\(\blacktriangleright\) Reliable judgment under adversarial search.}
JailbreakBench \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench} documents disagreement among automated judges, while CoinFlipJudge \cite{schwinn2026coinflip} shows that search can exploit judge errors. Separate judges for search and reporting, audit the reporting judge on current attacks, and route uncertain or high-risk cases to independent judges or human reviewers.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{\(\blacktriangleright\) Stateful and long-horizon testing.}
Many safety failures depend on memory, retrieval, tool state, authority, or a sequence of attacker choices. AgentPoison \cite{chen2024agentpoison}, MUSE \cite{yan2025muse}, and AgentDojo \cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} cover parts of this setting, but evaluations rarely combine persistent state, adaptive trajectories, executable actions, and auditable consequences. Tests should score full trajectories in environments that match the application risk.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{\(\blacktriangleright\) Transfer beyond the original target.}
GCG \cite{zou2023gcg}, multilingual jailbreak studies \cite{deng2024multilingual}, and PoEX \cite{lu2025poex} evaluate different transfer settings. Success can change with the model, language, modality, or deployed system, and harmful text, system behavior, and executable action require different success criteria. Studies should distinguish artifact reuse from strategy-level and failure-level transfer, then specify the source, target, and scoring rule.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{\(\blacktriangleright\) Comparing safety as evaluations evolve.}
Scores change when the target definition, cases, search policy, judge, or budget changes. HarmBench \cite{mazeika2024harmbench} and JailbreakBench \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench} stabilize parts of an evaluation, but systems and test procedures still drift. Retain anchor sets, version the system and evaluation configuration, and cross-evaluate selected old and new settings before claiming a longitudinal change in safety.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{\(\blacktriangleright\) Governing shared red-team evidence.}
External red-teaming practice \cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming} and coordinated flaw disclosure \cite{cattell2024coordinatedflaw} offer partial precedents for access and reporting. Shared repositories still need clear decision rights, maintenance duties, and response procedures for severe findings. Record provenance and versions for cases, judges, and attack artifacts, then define verification, provider notification, remediation tracking, and disclosure procedures.

\medskip

\section{Conclusion}

Automated red teaming should be evaluated as a closed-loop safety-evaluation system rather than as a collection of isolated jailbreak techniques. A useful survey must therefore connect risk specification, generation, search, interaction, judgment, coverage, mitigation, and responsible operational practice across generative-model targets.
