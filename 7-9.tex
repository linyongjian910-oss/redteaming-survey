\begin{abstract}
The rapid deployment of generative models has created a need for automated red-teaming methods that discover failures beyond manually authored test cases. This survey focuses on automated red-teaming systems that generate, execute, and evaluate adversarial or safety-relevant interactions with generative models and model-based applications. We study automated red teaming across large language models, vision-language models, text-to-image and text-to-video systems, retrieval-augmented applications, and tool-using agents. We organize the literature around a closed-loop pipeline that connects risk objectives, test-case generation, interaction and execution, outcome judgment, failure triage, and mitigation. Within this pipeline, we unify red-teaming strategies by their search space, optimization signal, access assumptions, interaction horizon, and modality. We then compare evaluation metrics, benchmarks, automated judges, human-in-the-loop protocols, and reporting practices, with particular attention to attack success, harm severity, coverage, transferability, cost, and over-refusal. Finally, we identify open problems in long-horizon interaction, multimodal and multilingual testing, reliable evaluation, adaptive coverage, reproducibility, and the responsible use of automated red-teaming systems. The survey is intended to provide a common vocabulary and a practical roadmap for building scalable, evidence-based automated red-teaming evaluations.



\end{abstract}

\subsection{Learning from Red-Team Failures}

Early automated red-teaming studies focused primarily on scalable failure discovery and characterization \cite{ganguli2022redteaming,perez2022redteaming}. More recent work connects these failures to model updates by reusing adversarial examples during safety tuning, expanding the distribution of attacks used for training, and generating new attacks against updated targets \cite{ge2024mart,lee2025diverseattacks,zhao2025diverct,mazeika2024harmbench}. These developments move remediation from isolated repair toward an iterative process in which red-team generation also determines part of the safety-training distribution.

\noindent\textbf{Training on Failures.} A direct approach converts successful red-team cases into safety supervision. MART \cite{ge2024mart} constructs safety-aligned data around adversarial prompts and fine-tunes the target model on these examples before the next attack round. This establishes a direct path from observed failures to targeted model updates. Once red-team traces enter training, the composition of those traces also becomes important.

\noindent\textbf{Diverse Attack Training.} Learning Diverse Attacks \cite{lee2025diverseattacks} trains red-team policies to discover attacks with different behaviors and reuses the resulting data for safety tuning. DiveR-CT \cite{zhao2025diverct} relaxes constraints during red-team generation to increase attack diversity and examines how the resulting attacks affect downstream blue-team tuning. Both studies treat diversity as part of the data-generation problem for remediation, extending the training set beyond repeated variants of the same successful attack.

\noindent\textbf{Re-Attacking Updated Models.} Diversity within one training round does not account for changes introduced by the model update itself. An attack that exposed the original target may disappear after tuning, while other weaknesses can remain or become easier to discover. MART \cite{ge2024mart} addresses this by alternating adversarial prompt generation and safety fine-tuning across multiple rounds, so later attacks are generated against the revised target. R2D2 in HarmBench \cite{mazeika2024harmbench} follows a related adversarial-training setting in which persistent test cases are dynamically optimized during training. The common design choice is to refresh the attack distribution as the target changes. This creates a stronger remediation loop than training once on a fixed collection of failures, because later red-team rounds probe the residual vulnerabilities of the updated model.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} Repeated remediation still does not establish robustness to attack strategies outside the training distribution. Simple Adaptive Attacks \cite{andriushchenko2025adaptive} reports high attack success on several safety-aligned models when attacks are adapted to the target, while past-tense reformulation \cite{andriushchenko2025pasttense} shows that a simple change in attack form can recover substantial attack success after refusal training. Safety Reasoning with Guidelines \cite{wang2025safetyreasoning} further reports that direct refusal training can generalize poorly under out-of-distribution attacks. Training-time remediation therefore requires held-out evaluation on attack strategies and behaviors that were not used to construct the remediation data.

\subsection{Turning Safety Judgments into Runtime Control}

Training-time remediation cannot fully prevent failures caused by adaptive prompts and unseen attack strategies after deployment \cite{andriushchenko2025adaptive,andriushchenko2025pasttense}. Inference-time mitigation therefore introduces runtime interventions without modifying model parameters. Existing studies mainly explore two intervention points: input protection before generation and output protection after generation \cite{inan2023llamaguard,zeng2024shieldgemma,han2024wildguard}.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Input Protection.} Input-level methods intervene before generation by detecting harmful requests, controlling external contexts, or reducing sensitivity to adversarial prompts. Llama Guard \cite{inan2023llamaguard}, ShieldGemma \cite{zeng2024shieldgemma}, and WildGuard \cite{han2024wildguard} develop safety classifiers for detecting unsafe inputs and responses during deployment. However, direct harmful prompts represent only one class of runtime failures. BIPIA \cite{yi2025bipia} studies indirect prompt injection attacks and evaluates defenses against malicious instructions embedded in external contexts, while Spotlighting \cite{hines2024spotlighting} marks the provenance of external information to distinguish trusted instructions from untrusted data. Another line of work focuses on adversarial prompt robustness. SmoothLLM \cite{robey2023smoothllm} applies randomized perturbations to prompts and aggregates multiple outputs to identify unstable jailbreak attempts, while Erase-and-Check \cite{kumar2023erasecheck} removes parts of the input and verifies whether the remaining prompt still induces unsafe behavior. These studies extend input protection from explicit harmful-request filtering toward handling hidden instructions and adversarially structured inputs.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Output Protection.} Output-level methods intervene after generation by evaluating, revising, or filtering model responses. LLM Self Defense \cite{phute2023llmselfdefense} introduces a response-side examination mechanism that uses an auxiliary model to identify unsafe generations and request safer responses. Backtranslation \cite{wang2024backtranslation} further analyzes the underlying intent of generated responses by reconstructing the original request and uses this information to guide response correction. These methods introduce an additional verification step after generation and create a lightweight correction loop without modifying the target model. Recent studies extend output protection by coordinating multiple models or agents during runtime decisions. AutoDefense \cite{zeng2024autodefense} assigns different agents to intention analysis, prompt analysis, and response judging, then aggregates their decisions to filter harmful outputs. AutoJailbreak \cite{lu2024autojailbreak} explores automated combinations of pre-generation and post-generation defenses through a mixture of defense strategies. These approaches move output protection from single-model response evaluation toward coordinated runtime decision processes, while the reliability of individual judges and the independence between defense components remain important factors.

\subsection{System-Level Protection Beyond Model Alignment}

As LLMs evolve from standalone chatbots into integrated systems with tools, memory, APIs, and autonomous workflows, their security boundary expands beyond model parameters. Research on prompt injection, tool-integrated applications, agent memory, and agent-security benchmarks has found that adversaries can redirect a deployed system through malicious user inputs, instructions embedded in external content, poisoned persistent state, and compromised control paths \cite{perez2022promptinject,abdelnabi2023indirect,debenedetti2024agentdojo,chen2024agentpoison,zhang2025asb}. Safety behavior can also weaken after downstream fine-tuning of open-weight models \cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,yang2023shadowalignment}. Alignment therefore does not guarantee instruction integrity, authorized execution, or durable state integrity after deployment. System-level protection must control information flow, executable authority, and the consequences of agent actions.

The pattern therefore supports a progression from information and authority separation, through authorization and action checks, toward execution containment and recovery.

\noindent\textbf{Context and Information-Flow Control.} A system must keep untrusted content from redefining the task or gaining authority over later actions. At the prompt layer, \emph{Ignore Previous Prompt}~\cite{perez2022promptinject} documents goal hijacking and prompt leakage through crafted inputs. Greshake et al.~\cite{abdelnabi2023indirect} extend the threat to LLM-integrated applications, where instructions hidden in webpages or emails can redirect an agent that reads external content before calling tools or handling private data. AgentPoison~\cite{chen2024agentpoison} shows that an attacker can poison an agent's memory or knowledge base and thereby influence later behavior. These attacks expose two integrity boundaries: the context assembled for the current task and the state retained for future tasks. CaMeL~\cite{debenedetti2025camel} separates trusted control flow from untrusted data and uses capabilities to restrict how retrieved content influences program execution. A practical design should preserve the source and trust level of information through retrieval, memory, planning, and execution, then restrict the operations that untrusted content may trigger.

\noindent\textbf{Access Control and Permission Management.} Independent application controls limit what an agent can do after it interprets a request. IsolateGPT~\cite{wu2025isolategpt} mediates application execution through isolated interfaces and permissions. Task Shield~\cite{jia2025taskshield} checks whether a proposed tool action stays aligned with the user's task, while ToolSafe~\cite{mou2026toolsafe} detects unsafe tool invocations before execution and returns the judgment to the agent. AuthBench~\cite{do2026leastprivilege} finds that coding agents may seek unnecessary or sensitive permissions, even when extra inference-time reasoning is available. These methods treat a model decision and an application authorization as separate events. The agent may propose an action, but an independent control must decide whether that action is permitted under the user's task, the available capabilities, and the current system state.

\noindent\textbf{Runtime Monitoring and Intervention.} Pre-execution checks cannot cover the full trajectory of a stateful agent. Runtime controls should inspect tool calls, intermediate state, and emerging consequences, then block an action, seek human approval, or limit its effects when a policy condition fails. AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} and ASB~\cite{zhang2025asb} provide environments for evaluating such controls; ToolSafe~\cite{mou2026toolsafe} provides a step-level intervention mechanism. ToolEmu~\cite{ruan2024toolemu} enables safety testing in an emulated tool environment before actions reach live services. Cordon~\cite{chen2026cordon} explores semantic transactions that delay commitment and support rollback at defined boundaries. Existing work gives stronger support for blocking or isolating unsafe actions than for monitoring long trajectories and recovering from effects that have already crossed an external service boundary.

\subsection{Continuous Testing After Remediation}

Red-team evidence is tied to the model and system configuration under which the failure was observed. As these configurations evolve, previously confirmed failures may \textbf{disappear}~\cite{tang2026evojail}, safety failures may \textbf{recur} after benign fine-tuning~\cite{peng2026cwac}, and measured safety may \textbf{change} across downstream versions~\cite{khan2026safetydrift}, while renewed red-team search can \textbf{uncover new vulnerabilities}~\cite{zhou2025autoredteamer}. Continuous red teaming therefore requires both preserving past failures for later replay and reevaluating safety as the target system evolves.

\noindent\textbf{Failure Retention.} Reusing a red-team failure requires preserving enough evidence to reconstruct the original evaluation. \emph{To Err Is AI}~\cite{mcgregor2025toerr} draws reporting practices from an open-ended bug bounty, emphasizing the review process and the artifacts used to substantiate flaws. \emph{Lessons for Editors of AI Incidents from the AI Incident Database}~\cite{paeth2025lessons} shows why structured incident records support later retrieval and analysis when the cause or consequence of a failure remains uncertain. \emph{FLARE-AI}~\cite{longpre2026flareai} identifies system identification, reproduction steps, and evidence as triage-relevant reporting dimensions, then uses structured, machine-readable reports to support coordination. Together, these studies suggest that retained red-team data should connect an attack trace to the evaluated system, the observed failure, and the conditions required to reproduce it. Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} further shows how attack outputs can be reorganized into reusable evaluation cases and refreshed as models change.

\noindent\textbf{Safety Drift.} Retained failures are useful only if safety is re-evaluated as the target evolves. \emph{Safety Drift After Fine-Tuning}~\cite{khan2026safetydrift} evaluates 100 base and adapted models and finds that benign fine-tuning can produce large and heterogeneous changes in measured safety, with improvement on one evaluation sometimes accompanied by degradation on another. \emph{Preventing Safety Drift in Large Language Models via Coupled Weight and Activation Constraints}~\cite{peng2026cwac} further shows that benign adaptation can weaken previously learned refusal behavior, motivating methods that preserve safety-critical representations during model updates. \emph{SafetyDrift: Predicting When AI Agents Cross the Line Before They Actually Do}~\cite{dhodapkar2026safetydrift} studies agent trajectories in which individually acceptable actions accumulate into later violations, extending the problem from model updates to evolving system state. Continuous red teaming therefore needs to compare safety across versions and repeatedly test the updated model or system; an earlier safety result should not be assumed valid after a change.

\noindent\textbf{Continuous Testing.} Retained replay and renewed discovery provide two complementary ways to track safety after updates. AutoRedTeamer~\cite{zhou2025autoredteamer} continually integrates newly discovered attack strategies, while Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} refreshes evaluation artifacts as models and attacks evolve. AgenticEval~\cite{wang2026agenticeval} further explores self-evolving evaluation strategies during testing. Together, these approaches suggest that continuous red teaming should preserve historical failures for longitudinal comparison while periodically renewing the attacks or evaluation set. In practice, this also requires consistent execution, persistent records, and reusable evaluation workflows across changing targets and test conditions.

\subsection{Operationalizing Red Teaming with Evaluation Harnesses}

Independently implemented pipelines handle target access, test execution, scoring, and record keeping differently, making outcomes difficult to reproduce and compare across studies. Evaluation harnesses organize these recurring operations into reusable workflows~\cite{derczynski2024garak,lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect,promptfooDocs}. \noindent\textbf{Framework Capabilities.} An evaluation harness combines the components that turn a red-team configuration into an executable experiment. Target adapters expose model APIs or application endpoints through a common runner. garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} uses a plug-in structure that combines probes, generators, and detectors for security testing. PyRIT~\cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit} structures targets, attack orchestrators, converters, and scorers into a reusable workflow and supports multimodal testing. promptfoo~\cite{promptfooDocs} defines providers, red-team strategies, and automated assertions in configuration files. Inspect AI~\cite{ukaisi2024inspect} couples model interfaces and scorers with tools and sandbox environments, then retains evaluation logs that support rerunning and re-scoring. Attack components issue selected probes; judges convert outputs into decisions; traces preserve the prompts, responses, settings, and scores that produced a result. Tool and agent environments extend this chain beyond prompt-response pairs, while retained records support replay after a target, judge, or execution setting changes.

\noindent\textbf{Harnesses and Static Benchmarks.} Static benchmarks remain useful because fixed cases and scoring procedures support direct comparison under controlled conditions. HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench} and JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench} provide common behavior sets, attack artifacts, and protocols for this purpose. Their coverage remains bounded by predefined behaviors and artifacts, while target systems and jailbreak strategies change. \emph{h4rm3l: A Dynamic Benchmark of Composable Jailbreak Attacks for LLM Safety Assessment}~\cite{doumbouya2025h4rm3l} represents attacks as composable transformations and synthesizes new attacks against the target, illustrating why static or templated sets can miss evolving combinations. Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} distills attacks into updateable benchmark artifacts. Harnesses make it easier to replace attacks, targets, judges, and execution settings, then retain comparable records when rerunning an experiment. They cannot, by themselves, ensure broader coverage, prevent saturation or contamination, or discover every new failure mode. Fixed benchmarks, renewable benchmarks, and harnesses are complementary: the first fixes comparison conditions, the second refreshes test content, and the third executes, records, and reruns either form.



\subsection{Where Human Expertise Still Matters}

\noindent\textbf{Human Contribution.} The evaluation infrastructure discussed above can scale test execution and scoring, but human expertise is still needed to determine which risks and contexts should enter the test space. Participatory red teaming \cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler,weidinger-etal-2024-star} and localized testing \cite{rastogi2026goingplaces,chiu-etal-2025-culturalbench} are especially useful when predefined taxonomies do not fully represent cultural, regional, or application-specific risks. \emph{Adversarial Nibbler}~\cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler} uses open-ended human exploration to identify text-to-image risks outside predefined categories, while \emph{Going PLACES}~\cite{rastogi2026goingplaces} relies on local participants to construct and judge culturally and linguistically grounded test cases. \emph{STAR}~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star} incorporates human-defined risk rules into red-team testing, and practitioner studies \cite{alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical} show that choices in test data and scenario design affect which users, contexts, and interaction patterns are represented. Human input can also be used selectively when automated judgments are difficult to interpret: \emph{Build It Break It Fix It}~\cite{dinan-etal-2019-build} shows that dialogue safety judgments depend on conversational context, while \emph{DICES}~\cite{NEURIPS2023_a74b697b} and \emph{D3CODE}~\cite{mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code} document persistent disagreement across annotators and cultural backgrounds. Human expertise therefore contributes primarily by supplying risk definitions, contextual knowledge, and evaluation criteria that are difficult to obtain from automated generation alone.

\noindent\textbf{Machine Amplification.} Automation is most useful when it turns these human inputs into reusable and scalable testing procedures. \emph{AART}~\cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart} converts human-defined application risks into reusable recipes and then uses model generation to produce larger adversarial test sets. \emph{Bridging the Scale Gap}~\cite{quaye2026bridging} extracts reusable strategies from human red-team cases and applies them to expand text-to-image testing. Model-based red teaming in Perez et al.~\cite{perez2022redteaming} similarly demonstrates how automated generation can produce attacks at much larger scale, while also showing that different generation objectives lead to different trade-offs between attack success and diversity. Automated judges \cite{perez2022redteaming} can further screen large numbers of generated cases before more expensive review. In this division of labor, humans provide the risks, contexts, and testing directions, while automated systems amplify those inputs through repeated generation, execution, and initial screening. Human review can therefore be concentrated on context-dependent dialogue judgments \cite{dinan-etal-2019-build}, persistent annotator disagreement \cite{NEURIPS2023_a74b697b}, culturally structured evaluation differences \cite{mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code}, and cases requiring additional adjudication \cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star}.

\noindent\textbf{Access Control.} The practices in Table~\ref{tab:redteam_disclosure} suggest that access decisions should consider how easily an artifact can be reused for harmful behavior. We identify three practical questions. \textbf{(1) Artifact sensitivity.} Benchmark definitions, aggregate results, and evaluation code may support broad scrutiny, while directly reusable jailbreak prompts, exploit chains, or detailed reproduction procedures require stronger review when they substantially lower the effort needed for misuse \cite{ganguli2022redteaming,mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,yu2023gptfuzzer,quaye2024adversarialnibbler,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyexploit,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit}. \textbf{(2) Access level.} Sensitive artifacts can be released through graduated access, ranging from public release to vetted access or provider-only disclosure. Existing studies and practice reports demonstrate these options, but do not establish common criteria for moving an artifact from one level to another \cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming,qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,solaiman2019release,bullwinkel2025lessons100products,openai2023gpt4systemcard,mcgregor2025toerr,cattell2024coordinatedflaw}. \textbf{(3) Access process.} A mature access policy also needs rules for requester eligibility, approval, revocation, and subsequent redistribution. The reviewed literature provides examples of controlled access, restricted technical disclosure, and coordinated reporting, but a standardized access-control procedure for automated red-team artifacts remains underdeveloped \cite{paeth2025lessons,longpre2026flareai}.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting Standard.} Release restrictions should be accompanied by a minimum reporting standard that allows the reported safety claim to be examined. We suggest recording \textbf{(1) the threat model and target configuration}, including model or system versions and attacker access; \textbf{(2) the artifact status}, specifying which prompts, datasets, models, or reproduction materials are public, gated, or withheld; \textbf{(3) the evaluation procedure}, including judges, metrics, attack budget, and success criteria; and \textbf{(4) the disclosure process}, including verification, provider notification, and remediation status when applicable. Existing studies and practice reports describe subsets of these elements across public benchmarks, external red-team campaigns, restricted technical reports, and incident-reporting frameworks \cite{ganguli2022redteaming,mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,ahmad2025externalredteaming,openai2023gpt4systemcard,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyexploit,zhu2025teamsllmagentsexploit,mcgregor2025toerr,cattell2024coordinatedflaw,paeth2025lessons,longpre2026flareai}, but no common standard currently links artifact sensitivity, access level, and minimum reporting requirements. Developing and validating such a standard remains an open problem for automated red teaming.

\section{Open Problems and Future Directions}
\label{sec:agenda}

\subsection{Toward Open-World and Coverage-Aware Red Teaming}

Automated red teaming increasingly reaches beyond fixed harmful-behavior sets through standardized benchmarks, scalable adversarial generation, black-box search, and prompt diversification \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,li2024salad,ganguli2022redteaming,perez2022redteaming,mehrotra2024tap,chao2025pair,yu2024llmfuzzer,liu2024autodan}. Broader attack generation does not necessarily produce broader failure coverage, because syntactically or strategically distinct prompts can still exploit the same underlying vulnerability \cite{zhao2025diverct,tong2023multimon,weidinger-etal-2024-star,pugh2016qualitydiversity}. Coverage is therefore difficult to define when the relevant risk space is not known in advance. Future work should develop \textbf{(1) extensible risk-space representations} that preserve benchmark comparability while admitting unspecified failure classes, \textbf{(2) failure-level novelty measures} that distinguish superficial prompt variation from genuinely new vulnerabilities, and \textbf{(3) discovery-aware stopping criteria} that connect testing budget and marginal discovery to uncertainty about unseen risks \cite{chao1984unseenclasses,pugh2016qualitydiversity}. These directions treat coverage as a property of the red-teaming process, not only of a predefined test set.

\medskip

\subsection{Toward Reliable and Adversary-Resistant Evaluation}

Automated judges have become central to scalable red teaming, with recent benchmarks increasingly replacing refusal matching and simple toxicity detection with specialized classifiers, rubric-based LLM judges, and multimodal evaluators \cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench,souly2024strongreject,han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,li2024salad}. However, evaluator choice can substantially change measured attack success, while agreement with human judgments remains sensitive to evaluation criteria, output style, prompt formulation, distribution shift, and model-specific biases \cite{zheng2023mtbench,chen2024humansjudge,chen2025saferluckier,eiras2025knowthyjudge,thakur2025judgingjudges}. More importantly, judges themselves constitute an attack surface: adversarial phrases, manipulated outputs, and poisoned evaluator data can systematically alter their decisions \cite{raina2024robustjudge,tong2025badjudge}, while multimodal evaluation introduces additional disagreement when harmfulness depends jointly on text, images, or temporal context \cite{chen2024mllmjudge,ying2024safebench,lee2025elite,wang2025mmsafeaware,liu2025videosafetybench}. Future evaluation could therefore advance along three directions: \textbf{(1) calibrated and uncertainty-aware judging} that exposes disagreement rather than collapsing every case into a single label; \textbf{(2) adversary-resistant evaluation} that explicitly includes the judge within the red-team threat model and tests robustness under attack and distribution shift; and \textbf{(3) selective human validation} concentrated on uncertain, conflicting, novel, or high-severity cases. Together, these directions would shift automated judgment from a convenient scoring component toward a measurable and auditable source of red-team evidence.

\medskip

\subsection{Toward Stateful and Transferable System-Level Red Teaming}

Automated red teaming is beginning to move beyond isolated prompt--response failures toward multi-turn interaction, persistent memory, tool use, retrieval state, and dynamic environments \cite{yan2025muse,debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhang2025asb,zhan2024injecagent,ruan2024toolemu,chen2024agentpoison,zou2025poisonedrag,wang2025webinject}. Interactive agent benchmarks further provide environments in which outcomes depend on action sequences and changing application state rather than a single generated response \cite{zhou2024webarena,koh2024visualwebarena,xie2024osworld,liu2024agentbench,yao2025taubench}. However, these dimensions remain only partially connected: multi-turn dialogue does not necessarily involve persistent external state, stateful attacks do not necessarily require long trajectories, and failures observed in one system may not persist after the model, language, modality, or application boundary changes. Transfer studies likewise report substantial source--target dependence across models and representations \cite{zou2023gcg,liu2024autodan,liao2024amplegcg,deng2024multilingual,geng2025coninstruction,he2026tear}. Future work could therefore advance along three directions: \textbf{(1) trajectory-level search} over histories, memory, tools, and environment state; \textbf{(2) consequence-level evaluation} based on state transitions and external actions rather than final text alone; and \textbf{(3) structured transfer testing} that separates reuse of attack artifacts from transfer of attack strategies and underlying failures. Together, these directions would extend red teaming from testing model responses toward testing failures of evolving model-based systems.

\medskip

\subsection{Continuous Evaluation under System Drift}

Red teaming is increasingly adapting evaluation content and search strategies as models and deployment settings evolve \cite{zhou2025autoredteamer,zhang2025jbdistill,wang2026agenticeval}. At the same time, model updates, fine-tuning, system state, evaluators, and test procedures can each change measured safety \cite{tang2026evojail,peng2026cwac,khan2026safetydrift,dhodapkar2026safetydrift,mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. A longitudinal comparison is therefore hard to interpret when both the target and the evaluation change. Future work should establish \textbf{(1) versioned anchor evaluations} that isolate changes in the target configuration, \textbf{(2) living regression suites} that replay retained failures while periodically renewing attacks and test cases, and \textbf{(3) governed evidence repositories} that preserve provenance, manage sensitive artifacts, and support verification and disclosure \cite{paeth2025lessons,longpre2026flareai,ahmad2025externalredteaming,cattell2024coordinatedflaw}. Together, these directions would make safety evidence cumulative across model and system changes.

\medskip
\section{Conclusion}

Automated red teaming should be evaluated as a closed-loop safety-evaluation system rather than as a collection of isolated jailbreak techniques. A useful survey must therefore connect risk specification, generation, search, interaction, judgment, coverage, mitigation, and responsible operational practice across generative-model targets.
