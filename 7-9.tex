\begin{abstract}
The rapid deployment of generative models has created a need for automated red-teaming methods that discover failures beyond manually authored test cases. This survey focuses on automated red-teaming systems that generate, execute, and evaluate adversarial or safety-relevant interactions with generative models and model-based applications. We study automated red teaming across large language models, vision-language models, text-to-image and text-to-video systems, retrieval-augmented applications, and tool-using agents. We organize the literature around a closed-loop pipeline that connects risk objectives, test-case generation, interaction and execution, outcome judgment, failure triage, and mitigation. Within this pipeline, we unify red-teaming strategies by their search space, optimization signal, access assumptions, interaction horizon, and modality. We then compare evaluation metrics, benchmarks, automated judges, human-in-the-loop protocols, and reporting practices, with particular attention to attack success, harm severity, coverage, transferability, cost, and over-refusal. Finally, we identify open problems in long-horizon interaction, multimodal and multilingual testing, reliable evaluation, adaptive coverage, reproducibility, and the responsible use of automated red-teaming systems. The survey is intended to provide a common vocabulary and a practical roadmap for building scalable, evidence-based automated red-teaming evaluations.



\end{abstract}
\keywords{automated red teaming; generative models; large language models; vision-language models; text-to-image generation; AI safety; adversarial evaluation; jailbreaks}

\subsection{Learning from Red-Team Failures}

Early automated red teaming focused primarily on scalable failure discovery and characterization~\cite{ganguli2022redteaming,perez2022redteaming}. Subsequent work uses red-team outputs directly as training signals. Verified failure traces from red teaming provide adversarial prompts for generating safe responses, enrich fine-tuning datasets, and update attack sets as targets evolve~\cite{ge2024mart,lee2025diverseattacks,zhao2025diverct,mazeika2024harmbench,wu2025trident}. This connects red teaming directly to model updates, feeding discovered failures back into training data.

\noindent\textbf{Adversarially Derived Supervision.} The most direct way to use red-team findings for remediation is to pair adversarial instructions with safe responses and fine-tune the target model on these examples. For example, MART~\cite{ge2024mart} generates adversarial prompts and synthesizes corresponding safe completions, updating the model on this curated data. Similarly, TRIDENT~\cite{wu2025trident} generates harmful instructions and pairs them with safe demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning. In these approaches, prompts that successfully trigger failures become concrete supervision targets, giving the model direct examples of safe behavior on inputs it previously failed.

\noindent\textbf{Coverage-Oriented Data Design.} The success of this fine-tuning depends on how well the training data covers different attack types. Lee et~al.~\cite{lee2025diverseattacks} train red-team policies to produce prompts with varied behavioral profiles, using the resulting examples for safety tuning. DiveR-CT~\cite{zhao2025diverct} relaxes generation constraints to broaden the attack space available for defensive tuning. TRIDENT~\cite{wu2025trident} structures data generation across lexical variations, malicious intent categories, and jailbreak tactics. These methods aim to build training distributions that systematically cover model failure modes, moving beyond collections of individual successful exploits.

\noindent\textbf{Dynamic Adversarial Training.} Fine-tuning changes model behavior, altering the vulnerabilities that red teams need to find. MART~\cite{ge2024mart} alternates between attack generation and safety fine-tuning across multiple rounds, keeping new attacks focused on the latest model state. Similarly, R2D2~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench} maintains a pool of adversarial test cases that are optimized continuously as the model updates. These frameworks update attack generation alongside defensive tuning, finding new vulnerabilities as older ones are resolved.

While training-time remediation updates model weights, deployed systems must still handle untrusted inputs and dynamic context in production. Section~7.2 examines runtime defenses that inspect incoming prompts, guide decoding, and moderate generated outputs without modifying the underlying model.

\subsection{Turning Safety Judgments into Runtime Control}

Even after training-time remediation, deployed models remain vulnerable to adaptive attacks and previously unseen exploit variants~\cite{andriushchenko2025adaptive,andriushchenko2025pasttense}. Inference-time mitigation provides complementary defense by applying runtime interventions without altering model weights. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:section7_flowmap}, these interventions operate across three distinct stages: pre-generation input filtering, in-generation decoding control, and post-generation output moderation.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Input Guard.} Input guards examine or sanitize incoming prompts and retrieved context prior to model inference. Dedicated guard models, such as Llama Guard~\cite{inan2023llamaguard}, ShieldGemma~\cite{zeng2024shieldgemma}, and WildGuard~\cite{han2024wildguard}, evaluate prompts and outputs against predefined safety taxonomies to detect harmful requests, jailbreak attempts, and inappropriate content. To defend against indirect prompt injection, BIPIA~\cite{yi2025bipia} introduces boundary-aware representations and explicit reminder mechanisms, whereas Spotlighting~\cite{hines2024spotlighting} incorporates provenance encodings to demarcate untrusted external data from system instructions. Robustness-oriented defenses mitigate sensitivity to adversarial prompt modifications: SmoothLLM~\cite{robey2023smoothllm} generates randomized character- and token-level perturbations of an input and aggregates predictions across copies, whereas Erase-and-Check~\cite{kumar2023erasecheck} evaluates token-subsequences to provide certified robustness guarantees against adversarial token insertions. SemanticSmooth~\cite{ji2024semanticsmoothing} generalizes randomized smoothing to semantic-preserving paraphrases. Collectively, these approaches broaden input defense from isolated keyword or intent filtering to structural context integrity and certified adversarial robustness.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Decoding Control.} Decoding interventions steer the token generation process dynamically, avoiding the need to rely solely on pre- or post-generation filtering. SafeDecoding~\cite{xu2024safedecoding} modulates token probability distributions using safety-guided directional vectors during generation, whereas Reasoning-to-Defend~\cite{zhu2025reasoningtodefend} enforces an intermediate safety-deliberation step prior to emitting the final response. Jailbreak Antidote~\cite{shen2025jailbreakantidote} guides hidden representations at inference time to suppress the activation of harmful concepts. Although these strategies preserve the underlying model parameters, they frequently incur additional computational overhead, increased response latency, and delicate trade-offs between safety and general utility.

\noindent\textbf{\ding{224}\hspace{0.35em}Output Moderation.} Output moderation evaluates, filters, or rewrites candidate responses prior to returning them to the user. LLM Self Defense~\cite{phute2023llmselfdefense} prompts the model to self-audit its generated text for safety violations, whereas Backtranslation~\cite{wang2024backtranslation} infers the underlying user intent from the generated response to verify compliance. AutoDefense~\cite{zeng2024autodefense} distributes output screening across specialized, cooperating defender agents. AutoJailbreak~\cite{lu2024autojailbreak} provides a modular framework to analyze and coordinate dependencies across attack, defense, and evaluation pipelines, demonstrating how input- and output-stage defenses can be composed synergistically. While post-generation moderation introduces a critical layer of defense, its effectiveness remains bounded by the accuracy of the verification model and offers no recourse if an unsafe generation has already triggered external tool execution.

While these inference-time defenses establish multi-stage protection for text-to-text interactions, they do not inherently restrict tool execution authority, safeguard persistent memory, or isolate external side effects. Addressing these vulnerabilities demands system-level safeguards---encompassing information-flow boundaries, capability-based permissions, action validation, execution containment, and transaction rollback---which are examined in Section~7.3.

\subsection{System-Level Protection Beyond Model Alignment}

As LLMs evolve from standalone chatbots into integrated systems with tools, memory, APIs, and autonomous workflows, the relevant security perimeter extends far beyond the parameter space of the model. Studies spanning indirect prompt injection, tool-augmented environments, agent memory, and agent security benchmarks demonstrate that adversaries can hijack execution via untrusted user inputs, adversarial instructions embedded in retrieved external data, poisoned memory stores, and subverted control paths~\cite{perez2022promptinject,abdelnabi2023indirect,debenedetti2024agentdojo,chen2024agentpoison,zhang2025asb}. Furthermore, safety guarantees frequently deteriorate following downstream fine-tuning of open-weight models~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,yang2023shadowalignment}. Consequently, parameter alignment alone cannot ensure instruction integrity, enforce authorization policies, or protect persistent system state. Robust defense requires system-level architectures that regulate information flow, constrain execution privileges, and mitigate the fallout of unsafe actions.

Table~\ref{tab:system_protection_capabilities} categorizes representative defense mechanisms by architectural placement rather than empirical effectiveness. The majority of existing defenses focus on pre-execution prevention. Among the surveyed systems, Cordon~\cite{chen2026cordon} is the sole approach that provides transactional recovery via staged operations, explicit commit phases, and automated rollback. The overall trajectory reflects an architectural progression: beginning with information-flow separation, advancing to fine-grained authorization and action validation, and culminating in execution containment and reversible state recovery.

\noindent\textbf{Context and Information-Flow Control.} A secure system must prevent untrusted external inputs from subverting user intent or escalating execution authority. Perez and Ribeiro~\cite{perez2022promptinject} characterize goal hijacking and system prompt exfiltration through adversarial prompt injection. Greshake et~al.~\cite{abdelnabi2023indirect} formalize indirect prompt injection in application environments, demonstrating that adversarial payloads embedded within web pages or emails can hijack an agent prior to tool invocation or private data retrieval. Chen et~al.~\cite{chen2024agentpoison} demonstrate with AgentPoison that adversaries can corrupt the long-term memory or knowledge base of an agent, persistently compromising future trajectories. These threats reveal two fundamental security boundaries: ephemeral context assembled for the active task and durable state retained across sessions. StruQ~\cite{chen2025struq} enforces structural separation between instructions and data at the query interface, whereas Wu et~al.~\cite{wu2024fsecure} cast defense against indirect prompt injection into a formal information-flow control problem in the $f$-secure architecture. CaMeL~\cite{debenedetti2025camel} extends this principle to dynamic execution, isolating trusted control flows from untrusted inputs and enforcing capability-based access control on their interactions. Similarly, IPIGuard~\cite{an-etal-2025-ipiguard} binds the agent to a predetermined tool-dependency graph prior to ingesting external data, preventing injected instructions from altering the planned execution topology. Robust system architectures must therefore track data provenance and trust levels across retrieval, memory, planning, and tool execution, strictly circumscribing the privileges granted to untrusted content.

\noindent\textbf{Authorization and Action Validation.} Independent application-level monitors restrict executable actions following intent interpretation. Progent~\cite{shi2025progent} introduces programmable privilege policies, whereas MiniScope~\cite{zhu2025miniscope} enforces least-privilege scoping across tool invocations. Task Shield~\cite{jia2025taskshield} verifies whether proposed tool actions remain faithful to the original task assigned by the user. AuthBench~\cite{do2026leastprivilege} reveals that coding agents frequently request redundant or sensitive privileges, a tendency that persists even when augmented with extended reasoning. These frameworks fundamentally decouple model proposals from application authorizations: while the model may suggest an operation, an external reference monitor decides whether the execution is permissible based on user-defined constraints, granted capabilities, and the global state of the system.

\noindent\textbf{Runtime Monitoring and Intervention.} While static checks validate discrete actions in isolation, continuous runtime monitoring verifies that multi-step execution traces remain strictly within policy boundaries. Comprehensive evaluation incorporates tool arguments, execution outputs, prior action sequences, active privilege levels, and environmental state changes. ToolSafe~\cite{mou2026toolsafe} audits individual tool invocations against the interaction history, supplying real-time guardrail feedback prior to dispatch. MELON~\cite{pmlr-v267-zhu25z} re-executes agent trajectories with the user prompt masked; behavioral congruence between the original and masked executions serves as evidence that injected instructions---rather than legitimate user goals---are driving the system. GuardAgent~\cite{pmlr-v267-xiang25a} translates natural-language safety specifications into executable verification code to monitor agent actions, while ShieldAgent~\cite{pmlr-v267-chen25ae} reasons over action trajectories against formal policy specifications to verify safety plans. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate that runtime surveillance can operate at the granularity of individual invocations, historical traces, or formalized logic. Nonetheless, they remain predominantly preventive, focusing on intercepting operations immediately before an unsafe side effect is realized.

\noindent\textbf{Containment and Recovery.} Preventive detection alone cannot resolve failures once an action modifies environmental state or triggers irreversible side effects. IsolateGPT~\cite{wu2025isolategpt} establishes execution sandboxes that confine untrusted application components from the broader operating environment. Cordon~\cite{chen2026cordon} introduces task-scoped transactional semantics: internal state updates are buffered in shadow memory, external actions are staged, and full multi-step executions are validated atomically prior to commit, enabling clean aborts and rollbacks upon policy violation. Recent studies extend this lifecycle paradigm: SafeHarness~\cite{lin2026safeharness} specifies automated rollbacks and adaptive service degradation upon state updates, while AIR~\cite{xiao2026air} couples contextual incident detection with dynamic containment, environment recovery, and automated rule synthesis. Furthermore, benchmarks such as AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, ASB~\cite{zhang2025asb}, and ToolEmu~\cite{ruan2024toolemu} provide standardized testbeds to rigorously stress-test these mechanisms, although benchmark harnesses must not be conflated with production defenses. Overall, state recovery and fault containment remain considerably less explored than preventive filtering, highlighting an urgent need for resilient system designs.

\subsection{Continuous Testing After Remediation}

Empirical safety guarantees remain intrinsically coupled to the specific model weights, software dependencies, and runtime configurations under which vulnerabilities were initially assessed. Downstream fine-tuning can severely degrade safety alignment, even when adaptation corpora are strictly benign~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels,peng2026cwac}; safety degradation varies widely across model architectures and evaluation protocols~\cite{khan2026safetydrift}; and formatting differences across prompt templates can silently disrupt aligned refusal behaviors~\cite{lyu2024keepingaligned}. These dynamics necessitate continuous red-teaming pipelines that archive historical failures for regression testing while systematically probing targets as they undergo ongoing refinement.

\noindent\textbf{Failure Retention.} Reusing red-team findings requires preserving comprehensive provenance to reproduce the observed failure. Drawing from vulnerability bounty programs, McGregor~\cite{mcgregor2025toerr} emphasizes structured review workflows and concrete audit artifacts required to validate flaws. Paeth et~al.~\cite{paeth2025lessons} demonstrate that structured incident databases facilitate longitudinal analysis and retrospective auditing when the root causes or broader impacts of failures remain ambiguous. Furthermore, FLARE-AI~\cite{longpre2026flareai} identifies system metadata, deterministic reproduction traces, and empirical evidence as foundational reporting dimensions, utilizing machine-readable schemas to streamline triage and cross-team coordination. Collectively, these practices demonstrate that failure retention must bind the original attack trace to the exact target version, the observed safety breakdown, and the complete environmental configuration necessary for deterministic replay.

\noindent\textbf{Safety Drift.} Safety evaluations conducted on a base model do not transfer unconditionally to downstream adapted variants. Qi et~al.~\cite{qi2023finetuningalignedlanguagemodels} demonstrate that fine-tuning on even entirely benign data can compromise previously aligned safeguards. Lyu et~al.~\cite{lyu2024keepingaligned} establish that formatting templates used during tuning and inference substantially govern alignment retention, demonstrating that prompt wrappers constitute a confounding variable in longitudinal safety comparisons. Khan et~al.~\cite{khan2026safetydrift} analyze this phenomenon across more than one hundred model variants, revealing pronounced and heterogeneous safety drift where improvements on one benchmark frequently coincide with regressions on another. Addressing this fragility, Peng et~al.~\cite{peng2026cwac} show that benign updates erode learned refusal boundaries, motivating explicit weight- and activation-space constraints during adaptation. Consequently, continuous red teaming must track safety deltas across successive model versions through rigorous regression testing, rather than presuming that historical safety guarantees persist across updates.

\noindent\textbf{Continuous Testing.} Regression replay and renewed exploit discovery form two complementary pillars for sustaining safety assurance across deployment cycles. EvoJail~\cite{tang2026evojail} evolves attack generation dynamically to match updating target versions, whereas AutoRedTeamer~\cite{zhou2025autoredteamer} continuously assimilates newly emerging exploitation strategies into a growing test suite. Similarly, Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} distills candidate jailbreaks into modular, easily maintainable safety benchmarks, and AgenticEval~\cite{wang2026agenticeval} develops self-updating evaluation regimens for agentic systems. Synthesizing these methodologies, continuous red teaming must integrate regression testing against archived vulnerabilities with active, exploratory red teaming against the updated system. In production settings, achieving this parity demands deterministic execution harnesses, immutable audit logging, and reusable evaluation workflows capable of operating robustly across evolving model architectures and environment configurations.

\noindent\textbf{Harness Capabilities.} An evaluation harness turns a red-teaming strategy into an executable experiment by coordinating five core functions. First, target adapters connect to model APIs or application endpoints through a unified interface. Second, attack orchestration manages prompt selection, seed generation, query budgets, and interaction schedules. Third, automated judges evaluate model outputs, tool invocations, or terminal system states to generate structured verdicts. Fourth, execution traces record the inputs, outputs, runtime parameters, software versions, and intermediate scores behind those decisions. Finally, replay mechanisms re-execute test cases under original conditions or against updated targets, judges, and environments to verify findings and detect regressions. In Table~\ref{tab:redteam_harnesses}, Generate and Adaptive describe test-case synthesis and search. Judge and Multimodal specify supported interaction modalities and evaluation mechanisms, while Record and Replay indicate whether experimental evidence is preserved and reusable. An entry indicating support for Judge reflects an integrated scoring interface rather than guaranteed label accuracy.

\noindent\textbf{Representative Designs.} Existing harnesses reflect different operational trade-offs across these capabilities. For vulnerability scanning, garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} pairs security probes and generators with dedicated detectors in a plugin architecture. PyRIT~\cite{lopezmunoz2024pyrit} coordinates targets, orchestrators, prompt converters, and scorers into reusable workflows that support multi-turn and multimodal red teaming. For application testing, promptfoo~\cite{promptfooDocs} offers a declarative framework to configure model providers, attack plugins, and automated assertions. Inspect AI~\cite{ukaisi2024inspect} couples model interfaces with tools and containerized sandboxes, retaining detailed logs for retrospective auditing and re-scoring. These design differences stem from distinct testing goals. A simple campaign may only need single-turn prompt probes, whereas testing tool-using agents requires recording multi-step state transitions across external environments. In both cases, the harness establishes the experimental boundary and provenance records needed to make empirical safety claims verifiable.

\noindent\textbf{Attack Engines and Evaluation Suites.} Attack-generation frameworks such as GPTFuzzer~\cite{yu2023gptfuzzer}, APRT~\cite{jiang2024aprt}, AutoDAN~\cite{liu2024autodan}, and TAP~\cite{mehrotra2024tap} focus on synthesizing adversarial prompts and optimizing search. In the taxonomy of Table~\ref{tab:redteam_harnesses}, they serve as workload generators rather than general execution platforms. In contrast, evaluation suites and benchmark environments fix part of the protocol to enable controlled comparisons. For instance, HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench} and JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench} establish standardized harm categories, baseline attacks, and automated evaluation routines. Similarly, AgentDojo~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo} provides a simulated environment to test tool-using agents against prompt injection, and MM-SafetyBench~\cite{liu2024mmsafetybench} extends standardized safety benchmarks to multimodal models. This distinction clarifies how benchmarks relate to harnesses: benchmarks supply test inputs, threat models, and evaluation criteria, while harnesses provide the underlying software environment to run, record, and replay those tests.

\noindent\textbf{Static and Renewable Workloads.} Static benchmark suites remain useful because fixed test cases and scoring rubrics allow consistent comparisons across models and defenses. However, their coverage is restricted to predefined categories and templates, which risk becoming obsolete as defense mechanisms and attack strategies change. Addressing this limitation, h4rm3l~\cite{doumbouya2025h4rm3l} models adversarial jailbreaks as composable syntactic and semantic transformations, generating novel attacks that expose blind spots in static datasets. Similarly, Jailbreak Distillation~\cite{zhang2025jbdistill} extracts varied failure traces into modular, updateable benchmarks. Static suites, renewable workloads, and harnesses therefore serve complementary roles. Static suites provide stable baselines, renewable workloads refresh test content against updating models, and harnesses preserve the execution configurations and traces needed to run both over time. Even so, automated tools cannot decide which risks matter in a given deployment or resolve culturally ambiguous outputs. Those foundational tasks require human judgment, as discussed in the next subsection.

\subsection{Human Guidance and Automation in Red Teaming}

\noindent\textbf{Defining the Standard.} Automated red teaming requires an explicit operational standard that defines the goals of a campaign. This standard specifies which risks belong in scope, which system boundaries are evaluated, what constitutes harmful behavior or an invalid action, and what evidence is required to confirm a safety failure. These choices determine both the vulnerabilities that can be found and how reported results should be interpreted. STAR~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star} structures human involvement through parameterized instructions for red teamers, demographic matching between annotators and harm topics, and formal rules to resolve divergent judgments. In addition, an empirical study by Alvarado Garcia et al.~\cite{alvaradogarcia2026sociotechnical} shows that choices made by practitioners regarding risk taxonomies, deployment scenarios, dataset generation, and evaluation rubrics directly determine which user populations and cultural contexts are represented in red-teaming benchmarks.

\noindent\textbf{Revising the Standard.} Evaluation standards must also reflect the contexts where models will be deployed. Adversarial Nibbler~\cite{quaye2024adversarialnibbler} uses crowdsourced exploration to gather implicit adversarial prompts, identifying text-to-image safety failures that automated pipelines often miss. Going PLACES~\cite{rastogi2026goingplaces} works with local communities to incorporate regional cultural and linguistic norms into evaluation cases. Such human-centered efforts update the risk register rather than merely growing static benchmarks. They reveal unmodeled harm categories, overlooked system interfaces, and failure modes that cannot be diagnosed from isolated text outputs alone. Furthermore, DICES~\cite{NEURIPS2023_a74b697b} and D3CODE~\cite{mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code} document widespread disagreement in safety and offensiveness ratings across different demographics and cultures. Such disagreement provides a valuable signal that an evaluation rule needs explicit human review, rather than being collapsed into an oversimplified majority label.

\noindent\textbf{Automation as a Multiplier.} Automation helps scale these human-defined standards. For example, AART~\cite{radharapu-etal-2023-aart} uses modular evaluation recipes to define application-specific risk concepts and generate large adversarial test sets. Bridging the Scale Gap~\cite{quaye2026bridging} starts from human-written adversarial prompts and systematically expands them using sociolinguistic transformations learned from expert red teamers. Evaluation harnesses then execute these expanded test suites and record complete interaction traces for comparison and replay~\cite{derczynski2024garak,lopezmunoz2024pyrit,ukaisi2024inspect,promptfooDocs}. Although automated judges can screen large batches of responses, their outputs depend entirely on the specified rubric, observable context, and decision threshold. High-stakes or ambiguous cases still require human audit and adjudication~\cite{souly2024strongreject,han2024wildguard,thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier}. Human oversight is therefore a regular operational requirement rather than an occasional exception. Experts must set evaluation criteria, analyze boundary failures and evaluative disagreements, and update risk registers, escalation thresholds, and disclosure rules as new evidence appears.

\subsection{Balancing Transparency and Exploitability}

Automated red teaming produces sensitive artifacts, including jailbreak prompts, multi-step exploit chains, attack scripts, and test cases, alongside empirical safety findings about a target system. Responsible disclosure therefore raises two governance challenges: deciding which artifacts can be shared with specific audiences, and determining what documentation is necessary to ensure findings remain auditable. Solaiman et al.~\cite{solaiman2019release} frame model and artifact release as a staged process rather than an all-or-nothing decision. An analysis of external red teaming from OpenAI~\cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming} shows that access controls and documentation standards are campaign-level governance decisions. In an offensive cybersecurity setting, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} demonstrate this balance by withholding full prompts and step-by-step reproduction instructions from the public, while granting access to qualified researchers upon request.

Vulnerability reporting and cross-organization coordination address a complementary question: how findings can be examined, fixed, and reported without creating unnecessary security risks. Cattell et al.~\cite{cattell2024coordinatedflaw} treat independent verification and formal review as key parts of coordinated flaw disclosure. Similarly, Householder et al.~\cite{householder2024lessons} outline an end-to-end disclosure lifecycle for machine learning vulnerabilities, covering discovery, validation, coordinated remediation, and public release. Table~\ref{tab:redteam_disclosure} maps representative practices across these two operational areas, highlighting that responsible disclosure requires managing both artifact access and finding accountability.

\noindent\textbf{Access Control.} The artifact-handling practices in Table~\ref{tab:redteam_disclosure} do not follow a rigid release hierarchy. Instead, they reflect three practical choices regarding artifact sensitivity, recipient scope, and public disclosure. First, regarding artifact sensitivity, benchmarks and fuzzers such as HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench}, JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}, and GPTFuzzer~\cite{yu2023gptfuzzer} release code and test cases openly to support public scrutiny. By contrast, Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} withheld full prompts and reproduction steps to avoid lowering the barrier to misuse. Second, recipient scope can be restricted by vetting users. For example, Ahmad et al.~\cite{ahmad2025externalredteaming} describe tiered access for vetted researchers, while Fang et al.~\cite{fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack} share sensitive artifacts on request. Finally, public accounts can communicate safety findings responsibly without releasing actionable exploits, as illustrated by the high-level summaries in the GPT-4 system card~\cite{openai2023gpt4systemcard}. These examples show the value of documenting sensitivity assessments, access tiers, and release decisions for each artifact, even though universal disclosure rules remain an open problem.

\noindent\textbf{Reporting Standard.} Finding accountability is an independent requirement alongside artifact access control. A safety report must allow external auditors to evaluate what was tested, what evidence supports the finding, and how the vulnerability was handled. Complete reporting should capture the threat model and target configuration (including system versions, dependencies, and assumed attacker access), the evaluation procedure (including query budgets, judges, and success criteria), the availability of evidence and artifacts (distinguishing public, gated, and withheld materials), and the disclosure lifecycle (including validation, developer notification, mitigation, and publication timelines). Studies of bug bounties, such as McGregor et al.~\cite{mcgregor2025toerr}, highlight the importance of formal review workflows and supporting artifacts. Paeth et al.~\cite{paeth2025lessons} demonstrate that structured incident databases are essential for longitudinal search and analysis, while FLARE-AI~\cite{longpre2026flareai} provides a machine-readable schema for coordinated reporting. Together, these frameworks show what information should be preserved, though establishing a uniform reporting standard for automated red teaming remains an open challenge.

\section{Open Problems and Future Directions}
\label{sec:agenda}

The primary objective of automated red teaming is no longer simply to maximize the volume of successful attacks. An effective red-teaming framework must unify stages that are often evaluated in isolation: identifying critical risks, discovering failures under concrete system configurations, verifying that observed outcomes represent authentic vulnerabilities, and confirming that subsequent mitigations reduce residual risk without degrading general utility. Prior surveys organize this domain around end-to-end operational pipelines or around attack taxonomies and search algorithms~\cite{purpura-etal-2025-building,Lin_2025}. Building upon these foundations, this survey conceptualizes automated red teaming as a closed loop. The open problems outlined below focus on the structural links required to make this loop rigorous: adaptive coverage, decision-grade evaluation, system-level compositional testing, durable remediation, and governed evidence sharing.

\subsection{Adaptive Risk Coverage Beyond Prompt Counts}

\noindent\textbf{Coverage must be mechanism-level, not prompt-level.} Automated methods can generate and evaluate far more test cases than manual red teams, but the sheer volume of prompts, query attempts, or successful jailbreaks does not constitute a meaningful metric of risk coverage. A massive prompt collection may consist primarily of syntactic reformulations that exploit an identical underlying vulnerability. Conversely, a compact set of adversarial cases can uncover fundamentally distinct failure mechanisms, such as instruction hijacking via retrieved context, unauthorized tool invocation, or persistent memory poisoning. Although static benchmarks enable controlled and reproducible comparisons, their fixed taxonomy categories necessarily delimit the spectrum of risks that they can reveal~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. Automated red teaming therefore requires coverage metrics that rigorously distinguish superficial surface-form variations from distinct operational failure mechanisms.

One promising research direction is to maintain an active risk register throughout the red-teaming search process. Under this design, each confirmed vulnerability is indexed not only by input prompt and execution outcome, but also by provisional failure category, target boundary, interaction context, and reproducible execution artifacts. Clustering algorithms and novelty metrics can then be evaluated on their ability to partition distinct failure modes and causal mechanisms, rather than simply maximizing lexical or embedding diversity. Existing formulations of diversity-oriented red teaming and quality-diversity search provide valuable algorithmic foundations~\cite{zhao2025diverct,li2024rtpe,jiang2024aprt,wu2025trident,zhou2025autoredteamer}. The central open challenge remains establishing formal criteria to determine whether a newly discovered test case exposes a genuinely novel vulnerability mechanism rather than a known exploit under an alternative phrasing.

Risk coverage also fundamentally depends on how the threat landscape is defined. End users, domain specialists, and diverse community stakeholders frequently identify contextual harms and threat scenarios that escape static taxonomy definitions~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,quaye2024adversarialnibbler,rastogi2026goingplaces}. Participatory red teaming offers a structured mechanism for incorporating these human insights; however, automated expansion procedures must preserve the contextual nuances that made an initial human discovery salient when generalizing it across synthetic variations~\cite{weidinger-etal-2024-star,quaye2026bridging}. Consequently, red-teaming frameworks should document both the target risks explored and the provenance of those risk specifications. This framing characterizes coverage as a transparent, auditable account of explored threat boundaries rather than an unfounded claim of exhaustive safety.

\subsection{Decision-Grade Evaluation and Trustworthy Judges}

\noindent\textbf{A reported attack is only as credible as its evaluation protocol.} The evidentiary value of an identified attack depends directly on the fidelity of the evaluation mechanism used to classify it. While automated evaluators facilitate high-throughput testing, disparate classifier architectures, rule-based heuristics, and LLM judges frequently assign conflicting labels to identical target generations. Discrepancies between automated judges and human annotators are particularly acute in ambiguous, conversational, or culturally nuanced contexts~\cite{souly2024strongreject,han2024wildguard,inan2023llamaguard,zheng2023mtbench,chen2024humansjudge,thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier,eiras2025knowthyjudge,NEURIPS2023_a74b697b,mostafazadeh-davani-etal-2024-d3code}. Furthermore, adversarial search algorithms can inadvertently exploit evaluator vulnerabilities, optimizing outputs to bypass or deceive the judge rather than demonstrating an authentic target failure~\cite{raina2024robustjudge,tong2025badjudge,shi2024optimization}. Consequently, an empirical attack success rate cannot be interpreted as an absolute property; it remains strictly conditional on the specific evaluator, scoring rubric, and decision threshold applied.

Future research must treat the evaluation protocol itself as an integral component of the experimental artifact. To support verifiable safety claims, experimental protocols should explicitly define operational criteria for violations, including harmful content, unauthorized actions, privacy leakage, policy circumvention, and task subversion. Studies should simultaneously document the observability scope accessible to the judge, alongside the exact evaluator version, scoring rubric, prompt template, and decision threshold. Although human adjudication cannot scale across all generated interactions, targeted human oversight is essential to calibrate automated judges on high-stakes, borderline, and high-variance instances. Rather than reporting only aggregate agreement statistics such as kappa coefficients or raw accuracy, evaluations should systematically characterize the failure modes and distribution shifts where automated judges and human consensus diverge.

This evaluation challenge becomes significantly more intricate in agentic and multimodal \mbox{systems}. A text-only judge may classify an intermediate utterance as benign even when the surrounding execution trace precipitates an unauthorized tool invocation. Conversely, monitoring only final environment outcomes can obscure whether an observed failure originates from the core model, a third-party tool, an overly permissive access policy, or environmental side effects. Decision-grade evaluation must therefore align specific failure criteria with the precise system boundary under examination. The primary objective is not to engineer an elusive, universal safety judge, but rather to establish transparent protocols that delineate which empirical conclusions are warranted by the observed evidence and identify where remaining uncertainties persist.

\subsection{Stateful and Compositional System Testing}

\noindent\textbf{The unit of testing is a trajectory, not an isolated response.} Conventional red-teaming evaluations predominantly assess security over isolated prompt-response pairs. This stateless formulation proves inadequate when a model operates with external context retrieval, persistent memory buffers, autonomous tool invocation, or multi-turn state transitions. In such sequential settings, a superficially benign initial turn can steer intermediate reasoning toward a compromised state, where the resulting vulnerability manifests as an unauthorized system action or irreversible side effect rather than explicitly toxic text. Specialized agent evaluation benchmarks have begun to formalize these threat vectors, demonstrating indirect prompt injection, tool-mediated privilege escalation, and multi-step workflow subversion~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo,zhan2024injecagent,ruan2024toolemu,chen2024agentpoison,zou2025poisonedrag,wang2025webinject}.

The critical next step is to evaluate multi-turn trajectories as structured execution graphs. A complete test trace should log the user instruction, retrieved context, state of memory, active permission scope, tool calls, intermediate reasoning steps, and external execution consequences. This diagnostic structure makes it possible to determine whether a defensive intervention neutralizes a vulnerability by preventing unauthorized execution, by altering how the model interprets untrusted context, or merely by inducing premature task aborts. Structured logging also enables the assessment of operational containment and fault recovery, capabilities that remain inaccessible when evaluation relies solely on a binary text label at the final step.

Adversarial transferability also requires re-examination as a system-level property rather than an isolated attribute of attack vectors. An exploit pattern may transfer readily across standalone base models yet fail completely when integrated into an application with distinct retrieval pipelines, system prompts, authorization boundaries, or execution sandboxes. Conversely, a failure mode that appears specific to a single model may recur systematically across disparate applications that share architectural control flows. Future empirical studies should deploy factorial transfer matrices that systematically vary four independent axes: attack representations, underlying foundation models, alignment defenses, and deployment wrapper configurations. Such designs clearly decouple intrinsic model vulnerabilities from architectural weaknesses in the host application~\cite{liu2023houyi,wu2025darkfunction}. Furthermore, this methodology yields nuanced findings regarding cross-model vulnerabilities that far surpass traditional, pairwise source-to-target transfer rates.

\subsection{Durable Safety Improvement Under Changing Conditions}

\noindent\textbf{Safety gains must remain observable as targets and evaluation conditions change.} Establishing a credible safety lifecycle requires capabilities beyond simply replaying historical jailbreak prompts against updated model checkpoints. A local remediation may suppress verbatim test prompts while leaving the underlying failure mechanism unmitigated, decrease one category of hazardous responses at the expense of severe over-refusal on benign requests, or introduce secondary vulnerabilities in downstream contexts. Dynamic red teaming and adversarial fine-tuning partially mitigate this limitation by synthesizing fresh attacks against updated targets, while archived failure suites serve as regression benchmarks for longitudinal tracking~\cite{zhou2025autoredteamer,zhang2025jbdistill}. The fundamental challenge is demonstrating that an observed performance gain reflects a verifiable reduction in system risk rather than an artifact of shifts in the attack generator, evaluation judge, or execution protocol.

A robust continuous-testing framework must decouple four distinct sources of experimental variation: updates to the target model or host application, variations in the attack generator and seed corpus, modifications to the evaluation judge, and alterations in the execution protocol. A shift in any single dimension can independently distort empirical safety evaluations. Target revisions can alter safety boundaries following task adaptation~\cite{khan2026safetydrift,peng2026cwac}; regenerated attack suites expose latent vulnerabilities overlooked by legacy test cases~\cite{doumbouya2025h4rm3l,zhang2025jbdistill}; and updated judges frequently reclassify historical outputs~\cite{thakur2025judgingjudges,chen2025saferluckier}. Consequently, evaluation frameworks should archive versioned execution traces with sufficient telemetry to replay interactions under their original parameters and re-evaluate them under updated protocols. Differential analysis across these configurations confirms whether an observed vulnerability has been resolved, has mutated in severity, or has been rendered uninterpretable by protocol drift.

Furthermore, remediation research must evaluate both causal efficacy and practical operational trade-offs. Rigorous defense validation requires specifying the exact intervention locus, evaluating against held-out attack distributions not encountered during defense development, and transparently reporting operational costs, such as degradation in standard utility benchmarks, inference latency, human moderation overhead, and tool functionality constraints. Confirmed failure cases should be curated into permanent regression test suites; nevertheless, active vulnerability discovery must operate concurrently with regression testing, as historical replay tests only anticipated failure modes. This integration transforms automated red teaming from an episodic demonstration of attacks into a continuous process for quantifying and bounding residual risk.

\subsection{Comparable Evidence and Governed Disclosure}

\noindent\textbf{Evidence must be comparable without making dangerous artifacts freely reusable.} Realizing these research objectives requires standardized operational infrastructure alongside algorithmic innovations. Standardized evaluation harnesses, dynamic benchmark suites, and structured incident databases preserve the configuration parameters, full execution traces, scoring rubrics, and replay environments necessary for longitudinal and cross-target comparison~\cite{ukaisi2024inspect,zhang2025jbdistill,mcgregor2025toerr,paeth2025lessons,longpre2026flareai}. Publications must comprehensively document threat models, target system configurations, assumed attacker permissions, computational query budgets, evaluator configurations, success criteria, and artifact release tiers. In the absence of these standardized reporting specifications, empirical performance discrepancies between systems risk reflecting disparate evaluation protocols rather than authentic differences in robustness.

At the same time, scientific reproducibility must not require unrestricted artifact dissemination. Certain evaluation assets, such as aggregate performance metrics, abstract schema definitions, and testing harnesses, can be released openly to foster community scrutiny. In contrast, dual-use assets, such as high-probability jailbreak prompts, executable multi-stage exploit chains, and automated cyberattack scripts, substantially lower the barrier to malicious deployment. Future governance frameworks must establish tiered disclosure procedures that calibrate artifact sensitivity against specific recipient authorization levels and mandatory minimum reporting packages~\cite{solaiman2019release,fang2024llmagentsautonomouslyhack,cattell2024coordinatedflaw,householder2024lessons}. This mechanism enables rigorous external auditing of empirical claims while managing actionable exploit proliferation through gated repositories or coordinated disclosure channels.

\noindent\textbf{The objective is durable, inspectable safety evidence, not a larger attack count.} A mature red-teaming framework provides explicit evidence of which risk spaces were systematically probed, how evaluation outcomes were adjudicated, which architectural boundaries were implicated, how vulnerabilities were mitigated, and which components of the experimental trace permit independent verification. While fulfilling this standard requires substantial methodological discipline, it represents the indispensable foundation for automated red teaming to inform high-stakes safety decisions rather than merely cataloging isolated exploits.
\section{Conclusion}

Automated red teaming should be evaluated as a closed-loop safety-evaluation system rather than as a collection of isolated jailbreak techniques. A useful survey must therefore connect risk specification, generation, search, interaction, judgment, coverage, mitigation, and responsible operational practice across generative-model targets.
